Introduction
SCOTT M. GIBSON AND MATTHEW D. KIM
Pastor Jacobs is preaching from Genesis 6 on the flood narrative. He
wonders how he will deal with the connection of the acts of Noah to the
New Testament. “How does the historical narrative of the Nephilim and
Noah and the ark connect to the New Testament?” he wonders. Pastor
Lopez sits in her study pondering how to preach about Proverbs 3:5–6. “Do
I need to mention Jesus here?” she thinks to herself. Pastor Hobart is
preaching from the book of Esther. He is struggling to incorporate the name
of God when God’s name is not mentioned in the entirety of the book.
Pastor Chung puzzles over his study of Jesus’s imperatives in the Sermon
on the Mount. “Does Jesus’s message here include the laws of the Old
Testament?” he queries. “What do I do with this pericope? Should it
connect with other texts, too?” wonders Pastor Okafor.
Every preacher preaches out of an articulated or unarticulated
perspective. The perspective might be methodological, theoretical,
philosophical, cultural, sociological, or theological. Plenty of books have
been written on the methods of preaching. Other books explore the
theoretical side. Additionally, some focus on a philosophy of preaching.
Still others engage the cultural or sociological aspects of the task. This book
is about teasing out the theological presuppositions of approaches to
preaching. That is, we want to explore the hermeneutic that lies behind
one’s theology of preaching. We have chosen four hermeneutical
approaches to preaching: redemptive-historic, christiconic, theocentric, and
law-gospel. The authors for this volume represent a range of theological
voices and expertise. Bryan Chapell, former president and chancellor of
Covenant Theological Seminary and currently senior pastor of Grace
Presbyterian Church, Peoria, Illinois, represents the redemptive-historic
position. Abraham Kuruvilla, senior research professor of preaching and
pastoral ministries at Dallas Theological Seminary, presents the christiconic
(pericopal) theological approach. Third, Kenneth Langley, adjunct professor
of preaching at Trinity Evangelical Divinity School and senior pastor of

--- Page 11 ---
Christ Community Church in Zion, Illinois, articulates the theocentric point
of view. Finally, Paul Scott Wilson, professor of homiletics, Emmanuel
College, University of Toronto, details the law-gospel perspective.
One may ask, “Why these four perspectives? Are there other points of
view that are not included in this project? Why are these positions included
while others are excluded?” First, these four perspectives reflect the current
streams of thought in evangelical hermeneutics and homiletics. Second,
these positions represent distinctive points of view, and by placing them in
conversation with each other, we can discover the contours of their
differences and similarities. Third, this project is not able to engage every
nuance of hermeneutical interpretation for homiletics. The Pentecostal or
charismatic perspectives are not addressed here. Their hermeneutical and
homiletical positions are certainly worthy of exploration, but we have
determined to limit the study to the four presented in this book.
The experts will present their hermeneutical positions: redemptive-
historic, christiconic, theocentric, and law-gospel, respectively. The authors
will interact with the following four categories in relation to their
hermeneutical stance: a biblical rationale, a theological rationale, a
homiletical rationale, and an applicational rationale. After each major
hermeneutical perspective, each other contributor will respond from his
theological position. In the final chapter, the editors will assess and engage
with the hermeneutical and homiletical views presented by the authors.
One objective of this first-of-its-kind book is to present to our readers a
robust discussion on the theological/hermeneutical approaches to preaching.
Another goal is to encourage conversation among preachers who advocate
different points of view. Third, we want readers to be able to answer this
question: What can we learn from a theological tradition that is not our
own? Finally, for those who have yet to articulate their own theological
understanding of preaching, we intend for this book to provide a way to
help readers determine their own preferred hermeneutical lens.
Our hope is that Pastors Jacobs, Lopez, Hobart, Chung, and Okafor,
among many others, will be better able to determine and appreciate the
place that hermeneutics has in their homiletics. By engaging with the
hermeneutical positions presented by the authors in this book, preachers
will be better equipped to preach texts with hermeneutical sensitivity. We
also hope that our readers will grow in appreciation for others’
hermeneutical traditions.

Redemptive-Historic View
BRYAN CHAPELL
Historical Background
Fifty years ago, there would have been little demand for the discussions of
this book among evangelical preaching instructors or pastors. Few expected
or desired the discipline of biblical theology to cause a major rethinking of
our approaches to preaching. Though that discipline’s approach to unifying
all of Scripture around the message of the redemptive work of Jesus Christ
had once inspired church fathers, energized Reformation preaching, and
empowered great awakenings of the gospel in this country, the redemptive
train was off the tracks. Liberal theologians had hijacked key aspects of
biblical theology, making evangelicals skeptical or opposed to its use.
Then, the pioneering work of preaching instructors such as Sidney
Greidanus, Edmund Clowney, and John Sanderson reminded late-twentieth-
century preachers that the unity of Scripture could not be dismissed without
harming our understanding of its particulars. They pointed to early church
fathers who took seriously what the gospels say about “all the Scriptures”
disclosing the ministry of Christ (e.g., Luke 24:27; John 5:39). This insight
had been abused, in ways that are now obvious to us, by ancient allegorism
that sought to make Jesus “magically” appear in every Bible passage
through exegetical acrobatics that stretched logic, imagination, and
credulity. But Luther and Calvin, among others, recognized the abuses and
attempted to offer corrections.
Luther’s law-gospel distinctions and Calvin’s forays into unifying the
Testaments were imperfect but important excursions into unveiling the
redemptive message culminating in Scripture. The writings of Bullinger,
Oecolampadius, and Beza in surrounding decades helped refine and
systematize a scriptural perspective that should have set the standard for

--- Page 13 ---
redemptive interpretation in following eras. Sadly, Counter-Reformation
battles regarding the nature of the church, justification, and the sacraments
eclipsed the discussion of how the unity of Scripture’s redemptive message
should guide our preaching.
Later Dutch Reformers would revisit biblical theology and influence the
Puritans, who took up the discussion again through key thinkers such as
Jonathan Edwards. His quest for understanding how religious “affections”
were stirred by the grace of the gospel led to a proposal to write a history of
redemption that unified the whole Bible—a project short-circuited by his
premature death.
The dormant discipline stirred again through the writings of Geerhardus
Vos but declined quickly in evangelical favor as liberal theologians used
select aspects of biblical theology to undermine the veracity of Scripture.
They argued that just as the “trajectory” of Old Testament scriptures
pointed to a Christ beyond ancient expectations, so modern preachers could
point beyond the canon of Scripture to disclose the “spirit of Jesus” for new
concepts of faith and ethics. As a consequence, biblical theology was used
to dispense with the clear teaching of the Scriptures and to advocate novel
ideas beyond canonical boundaries. In essence, biblical theology became a
weapon of “Liberalism” in the early twentieth century’s
modernist/fundamentalist “Battle for the Bible,” and it became a perceived
enemy of conservative Christianity.
Only after evangelicalism gained firmer ground in the 1960s and 1970s
did key voices begin to remind the Bible-believing church of the far-
reaching implications of our conviction that the proper interpretation of any
text requires regard for its context. That context includes not only its
literary and historical setting but also its place in God’s redemptive plan.
Exegetical and doctrinal disciplines began to register the importance of the
organic unity of Scripture for sound interpretation, and these insights
inevitably affected our approach to preaching.
In the homiletics field, biblical theology proponents who had been crying
in the wilderness for decades found fresh advocacy in the sermons of
preachers such as Don Carson, Joel Nederhood, Sinclair Ferguson, John
Piper, Steve Brown, James Montgomery Boice, Skip Ryan, Tony Merida,
Jerry Bridges, Ray Ortlund, Joe Novenson, David Calhoun, Danny Akin,
Ray Cortese, and most notably, Timothy Keller. Some preached out of an
instinct for infusing grace into their messages; others had more

--- Page 14 ---
systematized approaches. Some were consistent advocates; others felt their
way forward more haltingly. But all contributed to a movement that has
now swept beyond any anticipated academic, denominational, or
generational boundaries.
Homiletics movements have since converged with currents in exegetical
and theological disciplines, so that it is almost unthinkable that a new
commentary on any portion of Scripture would fail to contextualize its
contents within the redemptive flow of biblical history. Now, even if
elementary preachers are unsure how to preach a particular passage
redemptively, they have sensitive antennae to detect sermons that are mere
moralistic challenges to straighten up, fly right, and do better.
Biblical Rationale
Perspective
The biblical theology movement in preaching has been driven by the core
understanding that a message that merely advocates morality and
compassion can remain sub-Christian even if the preacher proves that the
Bible demands such behaviors. By ignoring the fallenness of our world and
works that necessitate God’s rescue (Isa. 64:6; Luke 17:10; Rom. 8:20), and
by neglecting the grace of God that makes obedience possible and
acceptable (1 Cor. 15:10; Eph. 2:8–9), such messages subvert the essence of
the Christian gospel.
All other faiths teach that humans reach God by some measure of effort
or mental state, but Christianity’s unique claim is that God graciously
reaches to us because of our inadequacy. The Bible teaches that our
relationship with God is not based on what we do, but on what Christ has
done—our faith is in his work, not ours (Gal. 2:20). Thus, a textually
accurate description of biblical commands and ethical conduct does not
guarantee Christian orthodoxy. Exhortations for moral behavior apart from
the work of the Savior degenerate into mere Pharisaism, even if preachers
advocate the actions with selected biblical evidence and good intent.
Spirituality solely based on personal conduct cannot escape its human-
centered orbit though it aspires to lift one to the divine.

--- Page 15 ---
Process
But how do expository preachers infuse gospel essentials (i.e., how God
is rescuing us from our fallenness) into every sermon without
superimposing ideas foreign to many texts? Many Old Testament passages
make no explicit reference to Christ’s substitutionary, penal death or bodily
resurrection. New Testament texts abound that commend moral behaviors
with no mention of the cross, the resurrection, the Holy Spirit, or God’s
enabling grace. Can we really be “expositors” and bring out of a text what it
does not seem to mention? The answer lies in an old preaching axiom:
Context is part of text.
By identifying where a passage fits in the overall revelation of God’s
redemptive plan, a preacher relates the text to Christ by performing the
standard and necessary exegetical task of establishing its context. Following
the creation passages at the outset of Genesis, all of Scripture unfolds a
record of God’s dealings with a corrupted world and its creatures (Gen.
3:15). But the record does not merely recite historical facts. It reveals an
ongoing drama whereby God systematically, personally, and progressively
discloses the necessity and the detail of his plan to use his Son to redeem
and restore fallen humanity and creation itself (Rom. 15:4).1
The big story of Scripture moves through the stages of creation, fall,
redemption, and final consummation—God made everything good,
everything went bad, and, in God’s time, everything will be made perfect.
But until that time, all human history subsequent to the fall (including our
own history) unfolds within the context of God’s redemptive plan. The
Bible is the revelation of that plan, and no scripture can be fully interpreted
without considering that context.
Just as historico-grammatical exegesis requires a preacher to consider a
text’s terms in their historical and literary context, responsible theological
interpretation requires an expositor to discern how a text’s ideas function in
the wider redemptive context. Some meanings we discern by taking out our
exegetical magnifying glass and studying a text’s particulars in close detail.
Other meanings we discern by examining a text with a theological fish-eye
lens to see how the immediate text relates to texts, messages, events, and
developments around it. Accurate expositors use both a magnifying glass
and a fish-eye lens, knowing that a magnifying glass can unravel mysteries
in a raindrop but can fail to expose a storm gathering on the horizon.

--- Page 16 ---
Theological Rationale
Principles
In the introduction to his seminal volume Biblical Theology, Geerhardus
Vos outlined the principles that will keep preaching on track. He began with
the simple observation that “revelation is a noun of action relating to divine
activity.”2 All scriptural revelation discloses God. In its proper context,
every verse in the Bible in some sense points to his nature and work. Yet
because God is God, no single verse, no single passage, no single book
contains all we need to know about him. In fact, had God totally revealed
himself to our earliest faith ancestors, they would not have had the
theological background or the biblical preparation necessary to take in all
that God has since disclosed to humanity about himself. For this reason,
God’s revelation through biblical history is progressive, organic, and
redemptive.
PROGRESSIVE
Vos first observed that the redemptive message of Scripture gets clearer
over time. This progressive principle of biblical revelation does not imply
that early revelation in any sense contradicts what God ultimately reveals.
What Samson understood about God’s purposes was not wrong, but Paul
understands more. God unfolds the message of Scripture in such a way as to
allow his people to progress in their understanding of his nature and
purposes.3
ORGANIC
Says Vos, “The progressive process is organic: revelation may be in seed
form which yields later full growth accounting for diversity but not true
difference because the earlier aspects of the truth are indispensable for
understanding the true meanings of the later forms and vice versa.”4
Everything is tied together. God uses each verse, each recorded event, and
each passing epoch of biblical history to build a single, comprehensive
understanding of who he is. Even though an aspect of God’s revelation of
himself may not be in full bloom in some portion of Scripture, that does not
mean that the truth is not present in “seed form.” Earlier portions of

--- Page 17 ---
Scripture help prepare us to understand later portions, and later portions
clarify the purpose and meaning of the earlier. They help explain each other.
REDEMPTIVE
The seed form instruction in Scripture does not necessarily appear as a
propositional statement such as would appear in a systematic theology
textbook. Our understanding of who God is remains inextricably bound to
what he has done. Writes Vos, “Revelation is inseparably linked to the
activity of redemption. . . . Revelation is the interpretation of redemption.”5
This means that for us to expound any passage of biblical revelation
accurately, we must relate it to the unfolding message of humanity’s rescue
from our fallen nature and world.6
Goals
God’s revelation is not simply cataloging his attributes or constructing a
behavior manual. God is revealing himself through his interaction with his
people in the context of a redemptive history that displays his gracious
nature. In this sense, the entire Bible is Christ centered because Christ’s
redemptive work—in all of its incarnational, atoning, rising, interceding,
and reigning dimensions—is the capstone of God’s revelation of his
dealings with his people. No aspect of revelation can be fully understood or
explained in isolation from God’s redeeming work that culminates in
Christ’s ministry.7
Christ-centered preaching, rightly understood, does not seek to discover
where Christ is mentioned in every biblical text but to disclose where every
text stands in relation to Christ’s ministry.8 The goal of the preacher is not
to find novel ways of making Jesus appear in every text (we should not
need a magic wand or a decoder ring to interpret Scripture), but to show
how each text manifests God’s grace in order to prepare and enable his
people to embrace the hope provided by Christ.
Many times I have been asked whether this understanding requires the
preacher to mention Jesus in every sermon. I have great respect for those
who ask because they are conscience bound that a Christ-centered approach
should not force them to impose on Scripture what it does not say. I
consistently respond, “No, I do not think there is a biblical mandate to name
Jesus in every sermon.” Then I quickly add, “But why wouldn’t you?”

--- Page 18 ---
While there is an undeniable logic that well-taught Christians may
contextualize Scripture’s imperatives and instruction within a redemptive
worldview, so that we don’t have to “spell it out” for them every time,
pastoral realities teach another obligation. Even when we preach grace,
most people hear law. The human reflex is self-justifying and transactional
(i.e., “I’ll do what God requires, and that’s why he will be nice to me”). If
we fail to mention Christ’s provision this week, and next week, and the next
week, because the text makes no explicit reference to him, should we really
expect our listeners to maintain grace-filled patterns of thought?
Mentioning Jesus’s name is not magic. That’s not the point. We also
teach our people that prayer “in Jesus’s name” maintains biblical priorities,
but we don’t argue God will not hear a desperate “God help us” uttered as
an eighteen-wheeler veers toward the family car. Just because Jesus’s name
is not mentioned does not negate our prayers, but that doesn’t mean that it is
wise consistently to approach the Father apart from the ministry of the Son.
So also pastors should know that God can bless sermons that only insist on
more duties to perform and doctrines to affirm. But why would we adopt a
pattern of preaching that risks communicating that God is only concerned
about our greater performance and competence, when the redemptive
context of every text gives us such liberty and incentive to preach Christ?
Precedent
Jesus identified the redemptive focus of all Scripture when he walked
with the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, after his resurrection. There,
“beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he explained to them what was
said in all the Scriptures concerning himself” (Luke 24:27; cf. 24:44 and
John 5:39, 46). Jesus related all portions of Scripture to his own ministry.
This does not mean that every phrase, punctuation mark, or verse directly
reveals Christ, but rather that all passages in their context serve our
understanding of his nature and/or necessity.
What Jesus verbally said on the road to Emmaus was visually displayed
on the Mount of Transfiguration (Matt. 17). When the archetypal
representatives of the Old Testament law and prophets, Moses and Elijah,
appeared with Jesus, they showed that all preceding Scripture directs the
believer’s gaze to this One. Thus, Jesus is the apex and culmination of
Scripture’s testimony.9 The law and the prophets that precede and the

--- Page 19 ---
apostolic ministries that follow the work of the cross center us on Christ’s
ministry. Prophets, apostles, and the Savior testify that all Scripture
ultimately focuses on the Redeemer our God provides to rescue us from our
fallenness (Matt. 11:13–15). How then can we rightly expound Scripture’s
passages and not relate them to the grace that culminates in Christ?
Expository preaching is Christ-centered preaching.
Homiletical Rationale
Methods
A common concern about Christ-centered preaching is that it will lead to
the allegorical abuses of past eras, when preachers tried to make texts refer
to Christ by imaginative connections that had no expository foundation—
for example, claiming that the wood of Noah’s ark represented the cross of
Christ, or that the parting of the Red Sea prefigures the division of the sheep
and goats at Christ’s second coming. Lest we think redemptive
interpretation requires such illogical leaps, it is appropriate at this juncture
to begin identifying approaches that lead to more faithful exposition of a
text.
TEXT DISCLOSURE
A text may directly refer to Christ or to an aspect of his messianic work.
Jesus or his saving activity may be mentioned in a Gospel account, a
messianic psalm, an epistle’s development, or a prophetic utterance. In such
cases, the task of the expositor is plain: explain the reference in terms of the
redemptive activity it reveals. But, though many biblical passages
specifically mention Christ’s person and work, many more do not. What
other alternatives may preachers pursue to stay Christ centered in their
preaching?
TYPE DISCLOSURE
God’s redemptive work in Christ may also be evident in Old Testament
types. Typology as it relates to Christ’s person and work is the study of the
correspondences between persons, events, and institutions that first appear
in the Old Testament and persons, events, and institutions in the New

--- Page 20 ---
Testament that more fully express salvation truths.10 Debates have swirled
through the centuries over what constitutes a legitimate type and what
merely reflects an interpreter’s overactive imagination.11 Current research
into literary methods and structures promises to enhance our understanding
of biblical typology.12 The consensus already shared by most biblical
interpreters, who are committed to the authority of Scripture, is that where
New Testament writers specifically cite or unmistakably echo how an Old
Testament person or feature prefigures the person and work of Christ—as
with Adam, David, the exodus, the Passover, and the temple—a preacher
may safely use typological exposition.13
CONTEXT DISCLOSURE
Texts that specifically mention Jesus’s ministry or reveal it typologically
are few relative to the thousands of passages that contain no direct reference
to Christ.14 How can a preacher remain Christ centered and expository
when dealing with these apparently Christ-silent texts? When neither text
nor type discloses the Savior’s work, a preacher must rely on context to
develop the redemptive focus of a message.
A passage retains its christocentric focus not because a preacher finds a
slick way of wedging a reference to Jesus’s person or work into the message
but because the sermon identifies a function that the text legitimately serves
in the great drama of God’s redemptive plan.
This mature view of Christ-centered preaching warns preachers not to
believe they have properly expounded a text simply because they have
identified something in it that reminds them of an event in Jesus’s life and
ministry. When a preacher uses a geographical reference to a well in the Old
Testament to introduce a discussion of Jesus’s conversation with the woman
at the well, no real explanation of the original passage’s place and meaning
in redemptive history has occurred. The preacher has only engaged in a bit
of wordplay. The same is true when a preacher leapfrogs to the New
Testament from a feature of Moses’s law or an event in Israel’s kingship
simply because a detail in the account seems similar to something Christ
did.
Searching out mysterious or coded “messianic lights” in precrucifixion
texts in order to make some reference to the incarnation, atonement, or
resurrection of Jesus is not the expository goal of Christ-centered
preaching.15 Since Scripture as a whole is God’s revelation of his

--- Page 21 ---
redeeming activity in Jesus Christ,16 a preacher needs to demonstrate where
and how a particular text functions in the redemptive plan.
In its context, every passage possesses one or more of four redemptive
foci (that we need not keep strictly segregated for our understanding). The
text may be
predictive of the work of Christ
preparatory for the work of Christ
resultant from the work of Christ, and/or
reflective of the work of Christ
These categories do not exhaust the possibilities of how texts may reveal
the redemptive work of God, but they do provide dependable means of
exploration and explanation.
Predictive. Some passages predict God’s redemptive work in Christ by
specifically mentioning his coming person or work. Messianic psalms and
passages from prophetic and apocalyptic literature provide many examples.
A sermon from Isaiah 40 that offers comfort to God’s people without
mention of Christ’s coming plainly misses the future source of comfort the
passage identifies in its context. Interpreting Old Testament passages
without considering how their features anticipate Christ’s ministry actually
diminishes reverence for the organic nature of Scripture.17
Preparatory. The inspired intention of some texts that do not specifically
mention Jesus is to prepare the people of God to understand aspects of the
person and/or work of Christ. We would not understand many aspects of
Christ’s New Testament ministry if the Old Testament had not prepared us
with accounts of the activities of the prophets, priests, and kings who
preceded him. Exodus events, temple sacrifices, foreign adoptions, merciful
pardons, providential rescues, and a host of other features and exemplars
(both positive and negative) in narrative, precept, prose, and poetry tune the
hearts and minds of God’s people to receive the Redeemer’s work at its
appointed time.
By such means, Old Testament believers were not only to anticipate
characteristics of the coming Savior but also to discern their need of faith in
the Redeemer. Key to this understanding were the Old Testament accounts
that consistently reinforced the message of all persons’ inability to keep any
divine imperative perfectly (Gal. 2:15–21).18 Exposition on the law that

--- Page 22 ---
fails to make this point advances an implicit legalism and misses the
explanation that the Bible itself offers for God’s commands.19 People today
must recognize that neither a sophisticated understanding of a
commandment nor the most vigorous attempts to heed it will merit grace.
Comprehensive explanation of what God requires falls short of adequate
exposition if it fails to say why God set the standard.
Resultant. Scripture includes many instructions that can be mistakenly
preached as conditions for divine love and acceptance. Such preaching errs
not by detailing what God requires but by implying, if not directly stating,
that a relationship with God is a consequence of obedience.20 The true
gospel proclaims that obedience itself is a blessing that results from God’s
love for us. The love we have for him that is engendered by deep
apprehension and appreciation of his unconditional mercy (made available
through Christ alone) stimulates our desire and efforts in obedience. Still,
even this desire and ability to do what he requires is of his Spirit and is
never cause for behaving as though our actions put God in our debt (Rom.
8:5–13; 11:35–36; 1 John 2:15–16). Many passages that describe the
privileges or blessings of obedience cannot be rightly interpreted without an
explanation that makes them an ultimate result of what Christ has done in
us rather than a direct result of what we do (e.g., Col. 1:29).
Divine love made conditional upon human obedience is mere legalism,
even if the actions commended reflect biblical commands. The only
obedience approved by God is that which he himself enables and sanctifies
through the union with Christ he provides.21 For example, my prayers in
themselves cannot earn, deserve, or require God’s blessing. God will be no
one’s debtor (Job 41:11). God is pleased by sincere prayers, and he
promises to bless according to his purpose what is offered in obedience to
him. However, though my prayers may be the instrument by which God
blesses, the merit of my prayers is never the basis of his care. With their
mix of human motives and their reflection of my own frail wisdom and
resolve, my prayers could never by their own worth determine or demand a
holy God’s blessing.22
I pray not to gain or earn my righteousness, but as a result of the access
to the Father that Jesus provides for me (and allows me to use) by his death,
resurrection, and continuing intercession. Thus, the writer of Hebrews
enjoins, “Therefore, since we have a great high priest who has ascended
into heaven, Jesus the Son of God, let us . . . then approach God’s throne of

--- Page 23 ---
grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help
us in our time of need” (4:14, 16). God mercifully receives and honors
prayer humbly offered in love to him not because our prayers are
inordinately good but because he is surpassingly gracious.
The promised blessings of prayer (as well as the opportunity for
fellowship with God) encourage my obedience in offering prayer, but the
acceptance of the offering is a result of Christ’s ministry and not the
sufficiency of my sincerity or diligence. To segregate a scripture promise
regarding the blessings of prayer from mention of Jesus is to consign
Christian prayer to the same hopeless and self-righteous folly of spinning
prayer wheels and reciting incantations.
To preach matters of faith or practice without rooting their foundation or
fruit in what God would do, has done, or will do through the ministry of
Christ creates a human-centered (anthropocentric) faith without Christian
distinctions. Truly Christian preaching must proclaim, “There is now no
condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus, because through Christ
Jesus the law of the Spirit who gives life has set you free from the law of
sin and death” (Rom. 8:1–2).
Christ’s work unites us to him and releases us from the guilt and the
power of our fallen condition. Now what we do in faith as those whose
pasts he sanctifies, whose resolves he strengthens, and whose futures he
secures must be seen as a result of what he has done and is doing in and
through us (1 Cor. 15:16–17, 58; Phil. 2:12–13; 1 Pet. 4:10–11). Every
aspect, action, and hope of the Christian life finds its motive, strength, and
source in Christ, or it is not of Christ. The truths of Scripture that do not
anticipate or culminate in Christ’s ministry must at least be preached as a
consequence of his work, or we rip them from the context that identifies
them with the Christian message.23
Proper exposition does not discover its Christ focus by disposing of a
passage’s teaching or by imposing Jesus on a text. Christ-centered
preaching identifies the role of a text in the Bible’s full testimony of God’s
gracious character, instruction, and actions, which are ultimately manifested
in Christ (2 Cor. 1:20; Rev. 22:13).24 Expository preaching need not
mention Golgotha, Bethlehem, or the Mount of Olives to remain Christ
centered. As long as a preacher uses a text’s statements or context to expose
the theological truths or historical facts that demonstrate the ultimate

--- Page 24 ---
necessity of God’s grace, Christ assumes his rightful place as the focus of
the message.25
Reflective. The path to implicit aspects of the gospel of grace that are
embedded in every biblical passage does not require tortuous expeditions of
logic. When a text neither plainly predicts, prepares for, nor results from the
Redeemer’s work, then an expositor should simply explain how the text
reflects key facets of the redemptive message. This is by far the most
common tool for constructing Christ-centered messages when there is no
direct reference to Jesus’s person or work.
Gospel Glasses
A preacher who asks the following basic questions takes no inappropriate
liberties with a text: What does this text reflect of God’s nature that
provides redemption? What does this text reflect of human nature that
requires redemption? Asking what a text reflects of God’s nature and our
nature is not an unfair or unnatural approach to any biblical text. Such
questions do not impose the New Testament on the Old Testament or
overlay the text with prejudicial theology. Without doing damage to the
integrity, authority, and exegesis of a passage, these questions act as lenses
to form a set of gospel glasses that enable us to view every text
redemptively.
If we ask, “What does the commandment ‘You shall not steal’ reflect of
the nature of God?” we will ultimately discern that he is holy. If we ask,
“What does the commandment ‘You shall not steal’ reflect about me?” we
will ultimately conclude that the prohibition against ever taking anything
that is not our own marks each of us as a thief. The commandment reveals
that God is holy and that I am a thief. And to make matters worse, the Bible
says that if I have broken one commandment then I am guilty of breaking
all (James 2:10). There is a problem here that I, as a fallen creature, cannot
fix. That is why the apostle Paul said that even the Old Testament law leads
us to Christ (Gal. 3:24). So also will the spiritual instruction stated or
implied in every biblical command, historical account, or poetic
encouragement.
With our gospel glasses in place we will see “the great disproportion”
between our best works and God’s holiness.26 This will also enable us to
perceive our need for God to make some provision so that we can be

--- Page 25 ---
reconciled to him and/or can do what he requires. Gospel glasses used
throughout Scripture equip us to focus on the glimmers of grace
consistently reflected as God provides for those who cannot provide for
themselves.
These gospel glasses are not X-ray goggles that make an image or
reference to Jesus mysteriously emerge from behind some bush in every
biblical account. Rather, they enable us to see reflected aspects of divine
character that provide the grace of God ultimately manifested in the person
and work of Christ, and/or to see our fallen nature, which requires such
grace. If we consistently ask these two interpretive questions, then we need
never pretend that all texts specifically mention Jesus or ever deny that they
lead our hearts to him. As we demonstrate how every text reflects aspects of
or needs for God’s grace that are made plain in the fullness of time, we
honor the unity of Scripture, God’s progressive plan of redemption, and the
many ways that the Holy Spirit coordinates the whole Bible to reveal the
grace of the Savior and the futility of any other hope.27
As a reminder, these gospel glasses may reveal redemptive truths
reflected in seed form, as well as in mature form. Grace may be reflected in
a direct New Testament statement of Christ’s work through the cross and
resurrection (e.g., 1 Pet. 2:24). Grace may also appear in Old Testament
clothes woven from the fabric of persons and events that the Holy Spirit
uses to reflect the redemptive character of God. Whenever God is providing
for those who cannot provide for themselves (supplying food for the
hungry, strength to the weak, rest for the weary, pardon for the sinful,
faithfulness to the unfaithful, etc.), he is displaying the grace that will
reflect more fully in the ministry of the Savior. Yet whether a preacher
gleans these conclusions from the historical sweep of Scripture, from a
passage’s specific doctrinal statements, from a literary echo, or from God’s
relational interaction with his people, the redemptive themes must be
harvested lest preaching sow mere moral commentary and reap Pharisaism
as its inevitable fruit.28
Gospel Approaches
I have lingered on this discussion of how the grace of God that
culminates in Christ may be “reflected” in God’s relational dealings with
his people because I consider this to be both the easiest and most common

--- Page 26 ---
approach for redemptive preaching from all Scripture. When I first began
teaching the impact of biblical theology on preaching, this was not so. My
models were the pioneers who rescued the redemptive-historical method
from liberal theology that had held it hostage for a half century. Yet I
discovered that, although my students appreciated that big picture of
redemption, they struggled to know how to preach individual passages
every week (i.e., Did they have to go from Genesis to Revelation every
week, and how can you be sure about your interpretation of a particular
passage’s role in all of redemptive history?).
The historical approach has remained foundational for all in the biblical
theology movement,29 but approaches have become more diverse as the
movement has matured and its preachers have multiplied. Some whose
instinct and inclinations are more doctrinal have become skilled at
demonstrating how Scripture discloses redemptive themes in statements or
actions that illuminate or presage New Testament doctrines (such as
justification by faith being shown in the righteousness that was credited to
Abraham, or the mercy of God for sinners demonstrated in God’s
instruction for Hosea to keep taking Gomer back as his wife).30 Finally, a
category of redemptive preachers has become particularly skilled at
showing the unity of Scripture’s purpose by using literary motifs (images,
actions, or phrases that are echoed from the Old Testament to the New).31
Each of these approaches (historical, doctrinal, and literary) brings
wonderful insights and skills to the task of proclaiming the redemptive
thrust of Scripture. Our redemptive interpretation tool bag has gotten more
full and varied to serve the different personalities who preach and the
different kinds of passages that need to be preached. Still, I have watched
my students question and stumble a bit as they have tried to imitate the
methods of some of the great, contemporary practitioners of these
redemptive approaches. So, as I have commended the preaching and
approaches of all of these methods, I have also encouraged my students and
myself not to forget how easy it is for us to put on our gospel glasses. When
we use the lenses of those glasses to ask, “What does this text reveal about
the nature of God and what does it reveal about me?” we will readily see
and confidently explain how God provides for those who cannot provide for
themselves. In this relational interaction, the grace of God that culminates
in Christ’s ministry almost always beacons clearly and powerfully.

--- Page 27 ---
Theocentricity
Exposition is Christ centered when it discloses God’s redemptive nature
as our Provider, Deliverer, and Sustainer whether or not Jesus is mentioned
more than the Father or the Holy Spirit. Jesus is not the only member of the
Trinity to be named in a sermon because he is not the only member
mentioned in our biblical texts. We should not worry, however, that a Christ
focus diminishes the Father or the Holy Spirit.32 Everyone who “abides in”
the message of Christ’s ministry “has both the Father and the Son” (2 John
9 ESV). We gain “knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus
Christ” (2 Cor. 4:6 ESV; cf. Heb. 13:8). And the earthly mission of the
Spirit who proceeds from the Father is to bear witness to Christ (John
15:26).33 Focus on the Son does not obscure the Father, of whom Jesus is
the “exact representation” (Heb. 1:3); centering on Christ does not sideline
the Spirit, whose mission is to glorify our Savior (John 16:12–14).34
A Christ-centered message is automatically theocentric because it reveals
the glory of the Father and fulfills the mission of the Spirit. But the contrary
is not true—focus upon the Father without reference to his redemption that
is fulfilled in the Son, or focus upon the Spirit that does not honor his
purpose of bearing witness to Christ, damages our witness to the glory of
each member of the Trinity. As Geerhardus Vos taught, God’s revelation of
himself in Scripture is “inseparably linked to the activity of redemption,”
and revelation is “the interpretation of redemption.”35 The metanarrative
matters. The mission of God is to reveal his glory through the ministry of
his Son, whom the Spirit enables us to perceive and receive.36 Supposed
theocentric exposition of a text that does not refer to the redemptive
revelation that culminates in Christ truncates the text, misunderstands its
design, and diminishes the means by which the Trinity is most glorified.
Theocentric preaching necessitates christocentric commentary not
because fairness requires equal time for each person of the Godhead but
because any “Christian” sermon must exegete Scripture to show the reality
of the human predicament that requires divine solution and identifies that
solution.37 A focus on God’s redemptive activity alerts the human heart to
its necessity, reveals the gracious character of God, and inevitably brings
Christ’s ministry into view (John 1:1–3, 14, 18; 14:7–10; Col. 1:15–20;
Heb. 1:1–3).38
Without this redemptive perspective guiding our interpretation, any
number of other worldviews could be overlaid on Scripture that are contrary

--- Page 28 ---
to its central theme. With this perspective of God’s overarching redemptive
plan (and Scripture’s organic presentation of it), each person, precept, and
event in the biblical record assumes its proper role in faithful exposition.39
Preachers will not present biblical patriarchs whose conduct was often far
from exemplary as perfect models for listeners to emulate. The ancient
saints will be presented as God intended—hopelessly fallen creatures whose
faith and favor are entirely the product of God’s mercy and deliverance.40
Sermons on the law will not merely detail moral precepts but will show
the contemporary people of God what the standards were intended to teach:
the necessity of divine dependence as well as holy conduct (Gal. 3:24).41
Preachers will not inadvertently teach that God’s acceptance depends on our
righteousness, given that the Scriptures consistently demonstrate that the
law itself pointed to the need of a greater provision of righteousness than
human accomplishment.
Messages on the times of the judges and kings will remove the veils we
so often put over these leaders to shield their reputations from their glaring
flaws. Preachers will then more freely herald all dimensions of the biblical
characters, understanding that human weaknesses underscore the necessity
of a righteousness that comes from God.42
New Testament instructions on marriage, stewardship, church
relationships, and worship practices will not be allowed to function as
aberrant reinstitutions of Old Testament law, qualifying God’s people for
his approval. Instead, all biblical standards (whether presented by written
precept or human example) will function as God intends—guiding God’s
people into the paths that reflect his glory, promote their good, and satisfy
their souls as the natural outflow of loving thankfulness for what he has
done or will do on their behalf.
Grace-focused preaching that neglects the moral and ethical aspects of
God’s law that are a gracious expression of his character and care denies his
people the safe and good path he intends for their lives. The necessity of
applying these rules with the motivation and enablement of his grace should
in no way diminish the necessity of presenting God’s standards in our
sermons.43 In Christ-centered preaching, the rules do not change, but the
reasons do. Preachers encourage obedience as a response to grace, rather
than as a qualification for it—motivating and enabling application of God’s
Word with the redemptive resources of Scripture.

Applicational Rationale
Motivation
Often scholarly discussion of the necessity of Christ-centered exposition
terminates in a debate about hermeneutical preferences, but preachers
cannot end the discussion there. We must consider the consequences of our
interpretations upon the application of our sermons. Nowhere are the effects
of Christ-centered exposition more apparent than when preachers proclaim
the motivations for their sermons’ applications.44 There are two common
errors regarding biblical motivation: the first is when preachers do not
acknowledge a plurality of motivations in Scripture; the second is when
preachers do not recognize there is a priority of motivations. The plurality
of motivations discussed below reflects the priority of Scripture by the
order of presentation.
A LOVE FOR GOD
Jesus taught that the greatest commandment is, “You shall love the Lord
your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind”
(Matt. 22:37 ESV). The foundation of all obedience is a heart for God
(Prov. 4:23). We live to honor him because we love him; we draw near to
him because we delight to walk with him. Serving our Lord without love
for him, or with any other love exceeding love for him, dishonors God no
matter how “good” our behaviors are in technical conformity to biblical
standards or in comparison to others’ activities. Unless love for God
compels our service, our obedience does not glorify him.
The source of our love is God’s love for us (1 John 4:19). This is why the
grace of Scripture that culminates in the ministry of Christ is so central to
gospel preaching. Grace threaded through Scripture and lavished on us
stimulates the love in us that motivates true obedience to him. Love for God
makes our righteousness a gift we offer God in loving devotion for his full
provision for our sin (Rom. 12:1; Heb. 13:15). Without this appreciative
response, obedience cannot maintain its doxological intent and may, in fact,
become an improper response to the guilt we feel as a consequence of our
sin.
Faithful preaching surfaces guilt to break and drive believers to true
repentance. Yet for that repentance to be genuine and fruitful, a person must

--- Page 30 ---
yearn for and be convinced of the power and magnitude of God’s kindness
(Rom. 2:4). That is why the apostle Paul, who identified love as his greatest
motivation in ministry (2 Cor. 5:14), urges us to offer ourselves as living
sacrifices “in view of God’s mercy” (Rom. 12:1).
Grace defined according to the world—as a license to sin or belittle
God’s law—ignores the Bible’s perspective that grace compels the heart
renewed by the Spirit to want and to do what God wants (Rom. 8:7–15;
Gal. 5:16–17). Grace alone motivates us to deny ourselves and enables us to
live for God (Titus 2:11–12). This is because at the most fundamental level
of our being we consistently do only what we most love to do. Thus, it is
the aim of God to renew the affections of believers so that their hearts will
most desire him and his ways. This is as contrary to antinomian preaching
as heaven’s blessings are to Satan’s lies. The desires of new creatures in
Christ Jesus can only be satisfied by the truths of grace fulfilled in lives of
obedience. When preachers nourish these desires with love for God, new
affections drive out the desires of the world and thereby strengthen the will
to serve God rightly and well.45 In addition, when rich apprehension of
Christ’s love makes the fulfillment of God’s will our greatest delight, then
his glory becomes our greatest pleasure and compulsion.
A LOVE FOR OTHERS LOVED BY GOD
When an appreciation for God’s love despite our sin motivates our
obedience to him, then the need to establish our righteous standing by
comparisons with others dies. Love for God overflows into the desire to
please him by caring for others he loves. That is why the second-most-
important commandment, “Love your neighbor as yourself,” is “like” the
greatest command (Matt. 22:39). To love God with our whole being
necessitates sharing his heart for his people and priorities; not to love them
is not to love him (Matt. 25:31–46; Luke 10:27–37; 1 John 3:17; 4:16–21).
Pride, indifference, and judgmentalism cannot coexist with this love.
Christians associate with and aid the needy precisely because (1) grace
assures us that the destitute and undeserving are the objects of Christ’s
affection, (2) they have precisely the status we did prior to Christ’s claim on
us, and (3) his claim upon us means that we can “afford” such associations
without loss of heaven’s esteem. Only Christ-centered preaching produces
such fruitful confidence.

--- Page 31 ---
Personal resonance with the desires of God lifts the concern of Christians
from solely individual interests. The critique that Christ-centered preaching
creates egocentric Christians, who are concerned only for the benefits God’s
grace individually provides for them, is illogical and impossible.46 If we
love Christ above all, then we will love what and whom he loves. He loves
all that he has made (Ps. 145:9), his creation and his creatures, including the
unlovable, the undeserving, the despised, the destitute, the oppressed, the
poor, the orphan, and the widow in distress (James 1:27). Proper emphasis
on what the Lord has done for us and desires to be done for others raises the
eyes of believers beyond the walls of self to consider (and truly love) the
underprivileged, unprotected, and unrepentant for Christ’s sake.47 Christ-
centered preaching keeps the individual, corporate, community, and
creational priorities of the gospel before God’s people.
A PROPER LOVE FOR SELF IN CHRIST
The Bible consistently and in many ways motivates believers through
their desire to experience the blessings of obedience or to avoid the
consequences of sin revealed by a loving God. These rewards and warnings
of Scripture would be senseless if God did not intend for us to have some
regard for our own well-being. This self-regard is both proper and
purposeful in gospel-rich preaching. If we are precious enough to God for
him to provide his Son to purchase us from eternal death, then it cannot be
evil to consider ourselves precious. We are the children of God, coheirs
with Christ, the temple of the Holy Spirit, the citizens of heaven, brothers
and sisters of Jesus, robed in his righteousness, and will rule with him in
glory. If God loves us this much, then it must be proper to love ourselves as
well.
Biblical self-love is actually the antidote to morbid Christianity—the sick
versions of religion that measure devotion by constant feelings of guilt,
expressions of self-hatred, and unrelenting tears of shame. When the gospel
ceases to be good news, it is not the gospel. When it ceases to bring joy into
our lives, it ceases to be of God. That joy is also the medicine needed for
much that ails a generation sickened by the cultural air we breathe. In an
age when our common pastoral issues are the products of self-hatred or
situational despair (cutting, anorexia, suicide, risky behavior, body
sculpting, body revealing, chemical abuse, spousal abuse, internet escapism,
etc.), we should not hesitate to urge all God’s children to love themselves

--- Page 32 ---
because Jesus loves them. Self-love is not unbiblical when it serves Christ’s
priorities and does not supersede love for him. Self-love only damages us
when it is served without regard for Christ, or as though it is more
important than Christ.
This instruction does not mean that grace should deter us from
mentioning sin’s biblical consequences. Instead, we should present biblical
identification of sin’s consequences as the gracious revelation of a loving
Father who wishes for us neither to experience the consequences of our
rebellion nor to face the discipline he must dispense in order to turn us from
even more serious harm. As previously mentioned, if God did not love, he
would not warn.
Enablement
Why must we keep returning to grace-prompted love in faithful
exposition of Scripture? Is it not enough to show that God has enabled us to
understand and do what he requires (1 Cor. 2:12; Phil. 4:13)? Faith that we
are new creatures in Christ Jesus should provide us with the confidence that
we can do what God requires by relying on the power his Spirit has instilled
within us (2 Cor. 5:17; Gal. 6:15). In addition, the practices and disciplines
of the Christian life that confirm and build this faith are powerful means by
which God instructs and blesses our lives. But something is still lacking in
our messages if we have only proclaimed duties and doctrines that will
increase people’s competence and performance.
The missing ingredient becomes apparent if we will dare to consider this
distasteful question: Since we are no longer slaves to sin, why do we sin?
Already we have discovered that the Bible will not allow us to say, “I just
can’t help it.” The grace of God has freed us from the guilt and power of
sin. The apostle Paul clearly says, “Sin will have no dominion over you”
(Rom. 6:14 ESV). John echoes, “He who is in you is greater than he who is
in the world” (1 John 4:4 ESV). So why do we sin? The answer we hate is:
Because we love it.
If sin did not attract us, it would have absolutely no power in our lives.
The reason that we yield to temptation is that we love the sin. That’s why
James reminds us, “Each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by
his own desire” (James 1:14 ESV). The ultimate source of our
transgressions is always that “people loved the darkness rather than the

--- Page 33 ---
light” (John 3:19 ESV). Sensitive hearts may immediately object, “That
cannot be true because I love Jesus.” Such love may very well be true, but
the reason for our sin is that, in the moment of trial or temptation, we loved
the sin more. We loved the passion, the rage, the idol, the entertainment, the
person, the reputation, the reward, the satisfaction, and so on more than the
Savior. Love for the sin is what kept us from employing the power we
actually possessed to resist it.
So, if our love for sin is what gives it power in our lives, how do we
displace love for sin? The plain answer is: with a greater love. The gospel
answer is: with a surpassing love for Christ (Eph. 3:16–20; Col. 1:18). That
is why love for God is Christ’s greatest commandment, the foundation of all
the rest (Matt. 22:37–39), and the means by which God presents himself to
us (1 John 4:8). When we love our Lord above all other loves, zeal for him
exceeds all other passions. This is why we must proclaim grace so
consistently and passionately.48 This is why God threads his gospel through
the totality of Scripture. Grace is not some sentimental, schmaltzy substitute
for bold exposition; grace is the stimulus of love that is the gospel’s greatest
power. Overwhelming love based on an understanding of the sufficiency,
efficacy, and majesty of his grace makes us willing and able to obey God.
Transformation
Preaching that stimulates ever greater love for God drives the affections
of the world from the heart so that it beats ever stronger for God’s purposes.
This is how Scripture always motivates and empowers obedience. Even
Moses preceded the Ten Commandments with a recounting of God’s
deliverance, not only so that the Israelites would not believe that their
salvation had been by their hands but also so that their hearts would turn
toward God.49 Provision of divine redemption in the face of spiritual need
is the consistent message of Scripture and the chief means by which human
hearts flood with love for God that is power to obey his commands.
Awareness of the power of proclaiming the goodness of God not only helps
govern the priorities of preaching but also brings the joy to preaching that
will make it a sustaining privilege for a lifetime of ministry.
No precise formula should govern how preachers maintain a Christ-
centered perspective when applying biblical truth.50 However, when people
walk away from a message understanding that grace both motivates and

--- Page 34 ---
enables them to serve God, futile human striving and vain self-vaunting
vanish. Transformed lives result.
As a consequence, preachers should make God’s redemptive work
integral to the content, the motive, and the power behind all biblical
exposition.51 The goal of such Christ-centered preaching is not some novel
interpretation. The goal is to encourage preachers to see and proclaim the
relationship God establishes with his people so that they may glorify and
enjoy him forever. Preaching the grace of all Scripture that culminates in
Christ’s ministry turns God’s people away from themselves as the providers
of their present healing and eternal hope. This is the bottom line of Christ-
centered preaching: When a sermon is done, do people look to themselves
or to a redeeming God for their security? Only when they know to look to
God alone has a sermon been truly beneficial and biblical.

Response to Bryan Chapell
ABRAHAM KURUVILLA
It is a privilege to respond to Bryan Chapell. He has been a resounding
spokesman for good preaching and I continue to learn from him.
In my response to Chapell, and in my two other responses, I shall address
a few issues in a rather loosely structured, eyewear-themed outline. You
will, no doubt, observe in the essays that Chapell has his “gospel glasses,”
Wilson, his “bifocals,” and Langley, his theological “lens.” Not having
mentioned any optic device in my contribution, I am either totally blind or
have 20–20 vision, obviating, in either case, the need for any vision-
enhancing appurtenance! But my “glassy-eyed” colleagues have a point:
every interpreter—and that includes me—has a particular scheme of
interpretation (“glasses”).
Chapell slips on his “gospel glasses” to “mention Christ’s provision this
week, and next week, and the next week” (8)—that is, in every sermon.
“Why wouldn’t you [name Jesus in every sermon]?” he asks rhetorically
(8). Answer: Because the overwhelming majority of chapters in the Bible
do not.1 I contend that there is no hermeneutical constraint to mention
Christ in every sermon, for he is not in every text, no matter what the
rhetoric employed.2 That being said, there is, no doubt, a pragmatic
constraint to do so, for one does not know if every listener in attendance is
saved. So, the gospel should be presented—somewhere in the worship
service (not necessarily in the sermon), by someone (not necessarily by the
preacher), somehow (not necessarily in any particular format).
Regarding the content that he insists should be preached, Chapell’s
multiplication of terms is confusing: “messianic work,” “redemptive
activity/focus/plan/message/themes,” “gospel essentials,” “what Christ has
done,” “Christ’s ministry,” “Christ’s provision,” and so on. And Chapell
asserts that Christ-centered exposition is simply the disclosure of “God’s
redemptive nature as our Provider, Deliverer, and Sustainer” (20). Why
“redemptive nature”? Why is it not simply the Triune God’s nature—period!
—to be Provider, Deliverer, and Sustainer? The addition of that adjective is

--- Page 39 ---
confounding (at least to me) and tends to strip “redemption” of any specific
meaning. The nuances of divine action, not to mention the personhood
unique to individual members of the Trinity, are overridden by imprecision
(fuzzy “gospel glasses”?).
I was surprised to find that Chapell thinks “what Samson understood
about God’s purposes was not wrong, but Paul understands more” (6). So
why even bother with Samson? Why not go straight to Paul? This is a grave
misunderstanding of the function of Scripture, particularly of the Old
Testament. At least as far as pericope-by-pericope preaching is concerned, it
is somewhat beside the point what Samson knew, understood, or believed.
What needs to be attended to is what the author, inspired by the Holy Spirit,
was doing with the story of Samson (or Ehud or Barak or Gideon or
Jephthah or whomever). Each pericope is depicting, negatively or
positively, what the perfect Man would have done, thought, or said (this
would be a christiconic reading), and this showing is accomplished in
detailed, nuanced, and finely granular fashion in the text itself.
On that note, there is a perilous blind spot in biblical studies. The New
Testament’s use of the Old Testament is not the definitive interpretation or
decisive meaning of those earlier writings. Prior texts are often
imaginatively applied, obliquely referred to, and creatively redeployed in
the newer productions—as intertextual puns, allusions and illustrations, or
prophetic applications—to serve these later appropriating authors’ inspired
purposes and agendas. No doubt there are formal applicational
interpretations of the Old Testament in the New Testament (1 Tim. 5:17–18
comes to mind). But the majority of New Testament citations of the Old
Testament are not literary, historical, grammatical, and rhetorical
expositions of the prior texts. So, when those inspired New Testament
passages with their Old Testament borrowings are preached, one should
handle such infallible and inerrant New Testament texts (including their Old
Testament quotes) as their New Testament authors intended—that is, what
these authors were doing, with puns, allusions, illustrations, applications,
and all. And, likewise, when inspired Old Testament sources are preached,
one should address those infallible and inerrant Old Testament texts as their
Old Testament authors intended—that is, what those writers were doing.
For preaching purposes, this is what authors of Scripture are doing with
what they are saying in each pericope: calling God’s people to live
responsibly as their relationship with him demands—each pericope

--- Page 40 ---
addressing a particular and specific facet of life—so that they may be
conformed to the image of Christ, in the power of the Spirit. This is nothing
but a gracious divine invitation to live in God’s “world” with him. The
result is divine blessing upon all who walk with God in faith-filled
obedience, as the patriarch Abraham did (see Gen. 22:16–18, for the
consequent ratcheting up of his blessing—a contingent enhancement).
Chapell, though, is suspicious: “Divine love made conditional upon
human obedience is mere legalism” (14). I would only remind him of Jude
21 (“Keep yourselves in the love of God,” ESV)3 and add an appendix to
Chapell’s assertion: God’s love is unconditional, all right, but the
experience of that unconditional divine love (along with its attendant
benefits and blessings) is conditional. We can keep ourselves in the love of
God (by obeying in the power of God’s Spirit) or take ourselves out of it
(by not doing so). There are consequences (blessing) for the children of
God for obedience (though not salvation, which is by faith alone in Christ
alone), just as there are consequences (discipline) for disobedience (though
not loss of salvation).4 Indeed, in John 13:17 Jesus expressly exhorts his
disciples, “You will be blessed if you do them,” referring to his exemplary
actions in that chapter.5 All that to say, God is concerned with our holiness;
and this holiness, empowered by God’s grace, makes possible a closer walk
with God and the enjoyment of his blessings by those who are already his
children.
I appreciated Chapell’s focus on motivation for obedience, primarily
“love for God.” But he displays the signs and symptoms of another widely
prevalent malady. Levenson has cogently and convincingly argued that in
literature of ancient Near East treaties, “love characterizes something very
unsentimental—the proper stance of the lesser party toward the greater,” a
posture of committed obedience and service by vassal toward suzerain.
Similarly, to “love” God is to obey his commandments (Deut. 10:12; 11:1,
13, 22; 19:9; 30:16, 20, where love for and obedience to God occur in
parallel)—a covenantal love. “Love, so understood, is not an emotion, not a
feeling, but a cover term for acts of obedient service.”6 This is why love can
be commanded (Deut. 6:5). That is not to say that such obedience is devoid
of affect. But the fact remains that the obedience of God’s children and their
filial devotion to him are inseparably intertwined: submission with
intimacy, fear of God with love for God. As was on display in the narrative

--- Page 41 ---
of Genesis 22, it is fear of God + love of God that leads to Abraham’s faith-
filled obedience and the subsequent escalated divine blessing.7
In sum: Every Christian inherently knows that the Bible is about Jesus
Christ—it is christological. The question is: How is it christological? I
submit that the christiconic interpretive method is the one that best
proclaims the specific thrust of the particular pericope, that best positions
each text within the trajectory of the book, that best places the theology of
each pericope in the overarching canonical momentum of conformation to
Christ, and, not least, that best performs a christological reading of
Scripture—hermeneutically, spiritually, and practically.

Response to Bryan Chapell
KENNETH LANGLEY
Bryan Chapell has done in this volume what he did in his widely used
homiletics text: offered preachers a clear, careful case for Christ-centered
preaching. He does not want Jesus clumsily plopped into texts where he
doesn’t fit (this is not a straw man; I’ve heard such sermons). He does not
advocate hermeneutical gymnastics that land every sermon on Calvary
(again, I’ve heard sermons like this: the moves are bewildering, the
landings shaky). He acknowledges that Luke 24 doesn’t teach that every
verse in the Bible directly reveals Christ (though he still puts more weight
on the debated verses than I think Jesus meant them to bear).
But I remain unconvinced for reasons stated in my own chapter. Even if
redemption should be the privileged theological theme that governs
preaching, let’s be sure to name God as Redeemer and protect the gospel
from harsh-Father-versus-kind-Son misunderstandings. Even if the grand
narrative of the Bible should in some sense shape every sermon, I want to
insist that God is the main character of that story. His is the eternal decree,
his is the love that drew salvation’s plan, his is the initiative in sending the
Son, his is the power that raised Christ from the dead and put all things
under his feet. “To him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus
throughout all generations, for ever and ever!” (Eph. 3:21 NIV).
Nor am I persuaded that redemption must be harvested from every text;
the Bible has other important things to say. I’m not persuaded that the grand
narrative must shape every sermon; the Bible has smaller but still important
stories to tell. Chapell has elsewhere cited with approval Spurgeon’s
statement that every text leads to Christ and that he “will go over hedge and
ditch” to get to the Master.1 But even if the road leads to Christ (not God?),
why must we travel the whole length of the road every Sunday; is it not
enough to travel a mile or two? And why must we never linger to observe
what God is showing us at the “hedge” of this week’s text?
Chapell writes, “All scriptural revelation discloses God,” and “every
verse in the Bible in some sense points to his nature and work” (6). Yes!

--- Page 43 ---
God, not one person of the Trinity. In some sense: not always recounting
God’s big story, but often looking at a smaller episode. His nature and
work: not only grace and not only redemption.
Chapell says we can preach what does not seem to be in the text (like
redemption, grace, or Christ) if we recall that context is part of text: the
whole of the Bible becomes our text. I could endorse a more cautious claim:
keeping in mind the context of all Scripture, we won’t preach texts in a
manner that undermines redemption, grace, and Christ. We won’t preach
divine demand as if listeners could earn a relationship to God by obedience.
We won’t preach Old Testament texts as if Jesus had never come. (What
would that look like anyway? The very fact that non-Jewish believers are
gathered on the Lord’s Day to hear Israel’s Scripture establishes a Christian
context beyond doubt.) But this doesn’t mean that every time we preach, we
preach the whole Bible.
I agree with Chapell that “there is an undeniable logic that well-taught
Christians may contextualize Scripture’s imperatives and instruction within
a redemptive worldview, so that we don’t have to ‘spell it out’ for them
every time” (8), but I’m less sure about his qualifying suggestion that
because the human reflex is to hear even grace as law, we need to make
explicit reference to Christ’s provision every week. The reflex of many
Christians, particularly Christians in the West in our own time, is to excuse
godless lifestyles in the name of grace. Preachers in our context need to
make certain that “faith without works is dead” is heralded as clearly as
“salvation by grace through faith.” Not that I want to privilege James 2:26
as the underlying theological claim for every sermon any more than I want
to follow Chapell’s prescription for redemption in Christ in every sermon.
Letting each pericope have its say in its turn will result in balanced theology
over time. We can surely trust the sovereignty of God enough to believe
he’ll bring people back another week (or use another witness) to hear the
other important truths they need to hear; the whole saving work of God
does not have to take place in a twenty-five-minute sermon.
Chapell acknowledges that we need not necessarily mention Jesus in
every sermon but adds, “Why wouldn’t you?” (8). Well, maybe because
other parts of the worship service exalt Jesus, and I need every minute of
sermon time to say what the text at hand says. Maybe because Christ and
redemption were central to the sermon last week, but this week we need to
hear about the Father and sovereignty or about the Spirit and inspiration.

--- Page 44 ---
Maybe because Christ will be central next Sunday and the Sunday after
that: after all, a theocentric ministry will include many christocentric
sermons on texts that are centered on Christ. Maybe because nothing is lost
if Christ is not brought into the text under consideration if it is not about
him.
On a road trip I listened to four sermons John Piper preached on Job.2
The first three did not mention Christ at all, not once; the fourth only in
passing. Yet these expositions were Scripture-soaked, theologically rich,
and pastorally probing (not to mention passionate; after all, this was John
Piper). They brought human sin and suffering into encounter with God—
not one person of the Trinity, but God. I don’t know how you could ask for
any more from a sermon.

Response to Bryan Chapell
PAUL SCOTT WILSON
Bryan Chapell starts with redemptive history in biblical theology, and I start
with a theology of the bifocal nature of the Word. Though our approaches
are different and were arrived at independent of each other, we end up in
similar places, at least from my perspective. The kinds of criticism Chapell
says he has received for his Christ-centered approach, that of importing
Jesus to texts or letting people off too easily (the antinomian claim), are
akin to criticisms of my own approach: that it imposes law or gospel on a
text (whichever seems least obvious) or that its imposition of the gospel
minimizes what humans are called to do. He speaks of God’s relational
interaction or redemptive revelation in the sermon, and I speak of Christ
coming to us in the proclamation of the gospel. Both of us emphasize God’s
eventful movement or action over static information: he speaks of “creation,
fall, redemption, and final consummation” (5) where I speak of law/trouble
to gospel/grace as God’s redemptive event in the preaching moment. He
cautions against moralistic or legalistic preaching, as do I. We also agree, in
his words, that “aspects of the gospel of grace . . . are embedded in every
biblical passage” (16). He now uses a question to get at the gospel, “What
does this text reflect of God’s nature that provides redemption?” (16),
where my theme sentence asks by way of grace, “What is God doing in or
behind this text?” I now speak of theologically inverting the theme sentence
to discover the trouble, and identify what God is responding to, where he
asks (in what elsewhere he calls the fallen condition focus), “What does this
text reflect of human nature that requires redemption?” (16). We agree that
humans are entirely dependent upon God to do what is required by the law
and that sermons that preach only human duty fall short in being
anthropocentric.
Part of loving our neighbors is concern for their redemption, and another
part is caring for their other needs like food, health, shelter, and so on.
Chapell notes the latter, and I wonder if he struggles with this as I do. The
redemptive paradigm must speak to social sin and the evil that can reside in

--- Page 46 ---
social systems, sometimes despite good people. One of my students
humorously commented, “The gospel is about meeting people at the point
of their need. And sometimes deliverance is not only about being washed in
the blood from Calvary; it is about being fed with bread from the bakery.
Jesus fed the multitude until they were filled.” Speaking to social issues is
difficult: ethical matters are often controversial, and topics like racism,
poverty, stewardship of creation, and injustice are complex; they may need
accompanying study programs during the week; sermons on such issues
may need more time than is available; and social justice sermons thus tend
to be anthropocentric. In fact, attention to the circumstances of daily life
and the social contexts of listeners today can be argued to need more
attention in each of the contributions to this volume. Every Sunday presents
something worthy of lament. Sometimes the best we can do is allude to
social issues and find a story of someone who models the kinds of action
that God both calls and equips us to do. Perhaps by maintaining references
to such issues each week, preachers can develop over time a common
vocabulary and commitment.
I find it helpful to speak of two kinds of trouble and two kinds of grace.
Trouble on the vertical axis says you are guilty and requires the grace of
forgiveness and atonement. It judges and demands repentance. Trouble on
the horizontal axis looks at the world in its fallen state and at social and
other issues that have resulted because of the fall. Horizontal trouble
awakens one’s consciousness to the needs of others. It requires the grace of
“I am with you always” (Matt. 28:20) and requires the Spirit to overturn the
powers of this world—as in death defeated, the hungry fed, the lonely
restored to community, the addicted recovering, and so forth. This type of
grace motivates and empowers service. We preachers need a lot of help to
speak with sensitivity to joy amidst sorrow, healing amidst illness, progress
amidst setbacks, and so forth. Such nuance characterizes many expressions
of God’s saving actions in the present, though they are often countered by
principalities and powers.
Chapell says that “any ‘Christian’ sermon must exegete Scripture to
show the reality of the human predicament that requires divine solution and
identifies that solution” (21). I sometimes use that language, but it
overlooks the primacy of preaching people into a relationship with God in
Christ through the Holy Spirit. Problems secondarily find solutions. Some
critics see trouble and grace as the old problem/solution sermon form and

--- Page 47 ---
then dismiss it as just another form. I see the Four Pages in my approach
mainly as a grammar,1 tools in a homiletical theology that can apply to any
sermon form to enable the gospel to be preached, heard, and experienced.
Law and gospel for Luther and Wesley were how to do theology, not a
form for sermons. Chapell resists a formula, but what are the formal or
structural implications of his approach? Do sermons move to redemption?
Since the redemptive need and the aspect “of God’s nature that provides
redemption” (16) are both rooted in the biblical text but have different
thrusts, does he employ a separate application of each in one sermon section
of application? How does that work? Is there a proportional balance that he
recommends? If there are shifts from one to the other, how does he
conceive of that?
Chapell identifies a “what, where, why, and how” (23n44) needed for
application in expository preaching, along with three biblical motivations:
love of God, neighbor, and self. I need some help to know how these
motivations work. Are they appeals the preacher makes to encourage
behavioral change? Are they explanations to help people consider
theological implications of contemplated action? Is one of these three loves
something the preacher discusses in each sermon, or are they for the
preacher’s benefit to contemplate in relation to the biblical text as one seeks
to motivate? They are trouble (what we must do), thus Chapell adds
enablement to them, so that motivation becomes grace-prompted. I take this
to be akin to Calvin’s third use of the law, which is grace-filled, an
excitement to obedience. In my own approach, I lean to at least two times
of application, Pages Two and Four,2 because it is hard to encourage
obedience and speak of God’s empowerment of it, at the same time.
I am currently revising Four Pages of the Sermon, and I conceive of
motivation in yet another way. I have heard sermons that focus on both God
and grace but that seem to have no connection to the needs people bring
with them to church. The problem is not that the message is irrelevant; it is
that the preacher did not take time to be clear about relevance. Karl Barth
says the Spirit ensures relevance, yet I also affirm we assist or resist what
the Spirit does. I now elevate “one need” in every sermon to a status not
quite equal to the theme sentence. I suggest that preachers, early in the
sermon, name the need in the form of a question that the theme sentence
answers. The need serves the biblical word, not the other way around. The
need avoids theological jargon and uses language ordinary people might

--- Page 48 ---
use. It takes some practice to get it right. If the theme is “Jesus heals the
blind man” (John 9), I might preach to the need, “How can I find my way?”
If the theme is, “God justifies us by our faith” (Rom. 3:28), the need might
be, “How can I prove I am good enough?”

Christiconic View
ABRAHAM KURUVILLA
“How Do You Read?”
When an expert in religious law asked Jesus about what he had to do to
“inherit eternal life,” Jesus replied with a counterquestion: “What is written
in the Law? How do you read [it]?” (Luke 10:26). The latter is a question
that still reverberates over the millennia: How does one read and interpret
Scripture for application?
A few years ago, in a church I visited, I found a copy of a popular daily
devotional that can be seen in the foyer of many churches. Skimming
through its pages in an idle moment, I spotted a devotional on Acts 28. Paul
is shipwrecked in Malta. And he joins everyone else in helping out, picking
up sticks for a fire. So, the devotional recommended, we too should be
willing to do menial jobs in churches. Always be willing to do even the
lowliest job. Of course, the writer of the devotional conveniently forgot
about the viper that came out from the cord and bit the hapless apostle.
I, being the clever guy that I am, could use that part of Acts 28 to
recommend exactly the opposite: Never, ever do menial tasks, because—
who knows?—a venomous beast, usually of the two-legged variety, may
sink its fangs into you!
While Jesus refers to the law in his query to the expert in religious law
who asked him what he should do to inherit eternal life, reading for
application from any part of Scripture is an issue that has plagued the
church for millennia. It has been the gaping hole in every theory of
preaching. How does one read the Bible to find valid application? David
Buttrick, homiletics scholar, once said:

--- Page 50 ---
Many books have been written on “biblical preaching”; specifically on how preachers can move
step by step from the Bible passage to a sermon. . . . But in all such books there seems to be a
gap. There’s something left out in between. The crucial moment between exegesis and
homiletical vision is not described. The shift between the study of a text and the conception of a
sermon—perhaps it occurs in a flash of imagination—is never discussed. So alert readers are
left with the odd impression that we move from the Bible to a contemporary sermon by some
inexplicable magic!1
The lot of the homiletician is not easy: each week, this intrepid soul has to
negotiate the formidable passage from ancient text to modern audience—a
burdensome responsibility.2 Stanley Porter agrees: “The move from the
original text of Scripture, with all of its time-bound character, to theological
truths for life today is one of the most demanding intellectual tasks
imaginable”—a task that confronts preachers each time the Bible is
expounded.3
How is all the intricate detail in the historic text relevant to the modern
listener? The crux of the hermeneutical problem is the traversal from the
then of the text to the now of the audience: words written in an earlier age
are to be transposed in some fashion into a later era. How are we to read the
text for preaching and application purposes?
In this chapter I will propose what I call a christiconic interpretation of
the text. I will approach the issue by exemplifying biblical readings for
application, and then proceed to detail the linguistic, theological,
applicational, and homiletical concepts that undergird such readings.
Biblical Rationale
A couple of biblical examples first—both from the Old Testament—will
depict a way of reading texts to arrive at valid application specific for the
pericope under consideration.4
1 Samuel 15
Take the narrative in 1 Samuel 15 (the story of Saul being ordered by
God to kill all the Amalekites). The prophet Samuel passes on God’s
message to King Saul that he should annihilate these peoples: “Listen to the
voice [קוֹל ,qol] of the word of Yahweh” (15:1). Sadly, we do not find
“voice” in most of our English Bibles; such a literal translation of the

--- Page 51 ---
Hebrew is found only in the King James Version and its heirs. The seeming
redundancy of “voice” is swept under the rug in all other major English
translations, which essentially have “Listen to the word of Yahweh.” Hold
that thought.
Saul, as we know, does not obey: rather than eliminate all the animals
and humans as commanded, he saves the good ones of the former and the
chief of the latter, likely as trophies for personal enrichment and glory. Soon
after, Samuel confronts Saul. The king declares he has done everything that
God told him to do. Whereupon Samuel says, “What then is this bleating of
the sheep in my ears, and the lowing of oxen which I hear?” (15:14).
But it is not “bleating” and “lowing” in the Hebrew—it is “voice” (קוֹל.)
With this “voice” (of animals, 15:14) and the earlier “voice” (of God, 15:1),
the author is doing something with this narrative, telling readers that the one
committed to God listens to the voice of God, not the voice of worldly
seductions. Again, most English translations render “voice” in each case
here as “bleating” and “lowing,” respectively, and thus, when this choice is
combined with the omission of “voice” in translations of 15:1, the force of
the text is almost completely negated! The author’s doing with what he is
saying is sorely attenuated by a misguided enthusiasm for what actually
happened behind the text—bleating and lowing—and, perhaps, for an
idiomatic English rendition of those behind-the-text events. But “voice”
forms a critical motif in the whole story, the key to determining what the
author was doing in this pericope (see also 15:19, 20, 22, 24). These
missteps are a clear indication that Bible translators and scholars do not
think in terms of what biblical authors are doing with what they are saying.
And here in 1 Samuel 15, the thrust of the text is clearly the issue of
listening to and obeying God.
The Aqedah
Here is another example of authors’ doings—the Aqedah, in Genesis 22.5

(Gen. 12:1; 22:2). Both stress a journey, an altar, and promised blessings. In

he took along his nephew, Lot, even though the divine word called for a

sixty-five (12:4)?
Soon after, as Abraham steps into the Negev, his caravan is hit by a

fear for his own life (20:11), again doubting God’s promises.
Thus, all along, Abraham is seen rather clumsily stumbling along in his
faith in God’s Word.6 Genesis 12–20, then, is not exactly an account of
pristine faith on the part of the patriarch.7 And, now in Genesis 22,
Abraham is tested: Would Abraham trust God after all that he had gone

The threefold description of Isaac in Genesis 22:2 was to emphasize that
this son, this particular child, was the one Abraham loved. And surely it is
significant that this is the first time the word “love” (אהב ,hv) occurs in the
Bible. This love of Abraham for Isaac was a crucial element in the test, a
love that potentially stood in the way of his fear of, and faith in, God. The
subsequent post-test deletion of the phrase “whom you love” clearly
indicated that Abraham had passed the examination. In sum, the test proved
the patriarch’s absolute allegiance to God—his unadulterated love for the
deity over and against anything that advanced a rival claim to that love: fear
of God.9 Nothing would stand between Abraham and God. And, in a
circuitous way, the text further confirms this.
An element of the account that has bewildered interpreters throughout the
ages is the disappearance of Isaac from the Abraham stories after Genesis
22:16. Indeed, the narrative itself concludes with, “So Abraham returned to
his young men, and they arose and went together to Beersheba” (22:19). No
Isaac? What happened to him? In fact, after this narrative, father and son
are never shown speaking to each other again in Genesis. After the test, it is
as if Isaac has altogether vanished. But the author is doing something with
what he is saying. A line had been drawn; the relationship between father
and son had been clarified; the tension between love (fear) of God and love
of son had been resolved. Now Abraham so loved God that he gave his only
begotten son. And to bring that home to readers, father and son are
separated for the rest of their days—literarily separated, that is, for the
purpose of achieving the narrator’s theological agenda: the love/fear of God
is to trump every other human allegiance! It is only by discerning this
theological thrust, the author’s doing, that valid application is possible:
being willing, ourselves, to give everything up for God.10
It is best to privilege the text and its immediate context to figure out what
the author was doing with what he was saying in this text. And such a
reading demands respect for the finely granular and brilliantly hued
particulars of the text, garnered by an exegetical undertaking that produces
tremendous yield for preaching and application purposes. In fact, this is true
of all pericopes I have looked at thus far, both in the Old and New
Testaments.11 One might interpret the Bible in many ways depending on
one’s goals for those interpretations. But when we interpret the text for
preaching (and life change)—and I emphasize that that purpose is the
exclusive concern of this essay—we must focus upon what the author is

--- Page 55 ---
doing with what he is saying in that particular text in order to elicit valid
application for readers. So what is this entity—the author’s doing with what
he is saying?
Linguistic Rationale
Recent studies in the fields of language philosophy and cognitive science
are deepening our understanding of communication and, particularly
pertinent for interpreters of Scripture, how texts are to be read.
There has been a long-standing tendency in the interpretation of
utterances to abide by a code model of communication that sees the
communicator’s thoughts as being borne in a socially constructed code (the
sentence) that, when decoded by a receiver, reproduces the communicator’s
thoughts in the receiver’s mind. The encoded message thus seems to exist in
a vacuum, autonomous and self-contained, suppressing authors and their
intentions.12 But, as we have been recognizing in the last five decades,
communication is, in addition, an inferential process. In other words, there
is a distinction between sentence meaning (i.e., semantics) and utterance
meaning (i.e., pragmatics: what the author/speaker is doing with what he or
she is saying).13 Decoding an utterance provides the former; an inferential
process yields the latter. Both are, of course, essential for interpretation.
For instance, if Mrs. A tells Mr. A, “The trash is full,” the sentence
meaning indicates the state of fullness of the trash can (the state of affairs
behind the text), while the utterance meaning exhorts Mr. A, “Take out the
trash!” (a projection in front of the text—see below). That is what Mrs. A is
doing with what she is saying. Pragmatics, an inferential process, uses the
statement of a speaker as an input and, with contextual information,
generates an output of the speaker’s intended meaning. Thus there is a
resulting division of labor: “semanticists deal with decoded meaning,” and
“pragmaticists deal with inferred meaning.”14 Both are necessary. But
sentence meaning typically underdetermines utterance meaning. In other
words, to catch what Mrs. A is doing with what she is saying needs more
than just the decoding of her sentence; it needs inference by Mr. A if he is
to grasp the thrust of his wife’s utterance. Unless he comprehends this thrust
—the pragmatics of Mrs. A’s utterance supervening in some way upon the
semantics thereof—there can be no valid application.15

--- Page 56 ---
All of this should cause the interpreter to be careful and nuanced about
interpreting texts—their very nature of conveying information must be
reconsidered. The semantics of an utterance (linguistically encoded
meaning, sentence meaning) is a template that must be enriched to arrive at
its pragmatics (inferentially discerned meaning, utterance meaning). Such
inferential operations are integral to interpretation, particularly
interpretation for application, which is every preacher’s burden (more on
that later).16 It is obvious that, in the interpretation of Scripture, authors’
doings are discerned almost exclusively from the written text, modalities of
conversational pragmatics being unavailable to distanced readers.17 But
when we’re dealing with sizable chunks of texts—say, for example, Genesis
22 in the midst of its immediate context of fifteen chapters (Gen. 11:27–
25:18, the Abraham story)—such difficulties are not insurmountable, as
was shown. The point is that it is only after discerning these doings by the
author(s) that one can proceed to valid application: taking out the trash (my
trivial example above) or listening to and obeying God’s voice exclusively
(1 Sam. 15) or holding nothing back from God, a wholehearted response of
faith, fear, and love toward an absolutely faithful God (Gen. 22).18 And, of
course, application is the end point of all interpretation of Scripture. Now
how does all this fit into a theological paradigm?
Theological Rationale
Application is what the texts of Scripture were primarily written for.19 In
the early second century, Justin Martyr, describing a worship service, noted
that after the reading of the Gospels, “the leader verbally instructs, and
exhorts to the imitation of these good things.”20 Augustine affirmed that the
aim of an expositor of Scripture was “to be listened to with understanding,
with pleasure, and with obedience.”21 Throughout church history, the goal
of Scripture interpretation was application. “The Bible,” Smart declared, “is
marching orders for an army, not bedtime reading to help one sleep more
soundly.”22 Scripture must be read for application, because Scripture is
God’s invitation to his people to live in an ideal world that operates by his
priorities, engages his practices, and is geared for his purposes. What do I
mean?

--- Page 57 ---
World in Front of the Text
A text is not an end in itself, but is the means to an end, a literary
instrument of the author’s action of projecting a transcending vision—what
Paul Ricoeur called the world in front of the text.23 Here’s the author of
1 Samuel (from the narrative of 1 Sam. 15): “Come, live in this ideal world
by listening to/obeying God exclusively!” And here’s the author of Genesis
(from Gen. 22): “Come, abide in this ideal world trusting/fearing/loving this
faithful God, holding back nothing from him—absolutely nothing!” In other
words, to live in those respective projected worlds is to adopt the values of
those worlds as proposed by those texts—heeding only God’s voice in one,
being utterly committed to God in the other.
Thus, in texts, a view of life, a world in front of the text, is portrayed, and
an invitation to that world is extended. Lives are changed as listeners
respond by inhabiting that world, abiding by its values. That is, a text’s
projected world-vision enables subsequent application. Therefore, the
interpretation of Scripture cannot cease with the elucidation of its linguistic,
grammatical, and syntactical elements: what the author is saying
(semantics). It must proceed further to discern the world in front of the text:
what the author is doing (pragmatics).24 And this projected world forms the
intermediary between text and application, enabling one to respond validly
to the text.
Indeed, all communication functions this way. For instance, if A tells B,
“Hey, you are standing on my foot!” the sentence meaning (what the author
is saying, semantics) asserts the spatial location of B’s foot upon the lower
limb of A, while the utterance meaning (what the author is doing,
pragmatics) attempts to get B to relocate that extremity from its traumatic
situation upon A’s anatomy. In fact, what A was implicitly doing was
projecting a world in front of the text, an ideal world in which no one is ever
stationed upon A’s foot to produce distress. A’s desire was for B to inhabit
such an ideal “nobody-ever-standing-on-A’s-foot-to-cause-A-pain” kind of
world. That habitation could be accomplished only by conforming to the
key value of that world—removing the burden off A’s foot, thus alleviating
the latter’s agony, for in A’s projected world in front of the text nobody ever
stands on A’s foot to cause A pain.

--- Page 58 ---
Unfortunately, that is not how biblical texts are interpreted in a homiletic
grounded upon the code model of communication. For instance, if that
statement by A to B (“Hey, you are standing on my foot!”) were an inspired
utterance in Scripture, a preacher in the traditional camp expositing that
“text” on Sunday morning would conceivably expatiate on the derivation of
the word “foot” from the Old English fot, and the Latin pes, and the Greek
πούς (pous), and the Sanskrit pād. The preacher might discourse upon the
foot’s kinesiology (twenty-six bones, thirty-three joints, over a hundred
muscles, tendons, and ligaments), its hematology (blood vessels), and its
neurology (nerve supply). This preacher would, no doubt, wax eloquent
about the pathology of that extremity (its various abnormalities: club foot,
flat foot, athlete’s foot, skew foot, rheumatoid foot) and so on, focusing on
all the “-ologies” but completely missing the utterance’s pragmatics and its
intended valid application: “Get your foot off mine!” In other words, unless
one catches what A is doing (pragmatics, the world in front of the text: an
ideal world in which no one stands on A’s foot to cause A pain), valid
application in response to A’s statement is impossible. All that regurgitation
of kinesiology, hematology, neurology, or one’s favorite “-ology” du jour
can never bring one to valid application.25
So also for the biblical text. The biblical canon as a whole projects a
world in front of the text—God’s ideal world, individual segments of which
are portrayed by individual pericopes. Taken together, the integrated
composite of all such segments is the canonical projection of God’s ideal
world in front of the text—the plenary ideal world.

--- Page 59 ---
Thus, each sermon on a particular pericope is God’s gracious invitation
to humankind to live in his ideal world by abiding by the thrust of that
pericope—that is, the values of God’s ideal world as called for in that
pericope’s world-segment. And as God’s people accept that divine
invitation, week by week and pericope by pericope they are increasingly
inhabiting this ideal world and adopting its values. “Attracted by it and
moved toward it, we begin to live into this vision of the good life and start
to look like citizens who inhabit the world that we picture as the good life.
We become little microcosms of that envisioned world as we try to embody
it in the here and now.”26 As we are captivated by the vision of God’s ideal
world, the various aspects of Christian life, individual and corporate, are
gradually—one pericope at a time—being brought into alignment with the
will of God by the power of God for the glory of God: God’s Word is being
applied, and God’s world is becoming reality. This is the goal of preaching.
Pericopal Theology
Because this world speaks of God and how he relates to his creation, and
because it bears direction for life change, this projected world may rightly
be called “theology.” Thus, the segment of this ideal world that each
pericope projects becomes the theology of that pericope—what I call
pericopal theology.27 To live by pericopal theology is to accept God’s

--- Page 60 ---
gracious invitation to inhabit his ideal world; and by so doing, his people
are aligned to the precepts, priorities, and practices of that ideal world—that
is, God’s will as portrayed by each pericope.
So, each sermon must point out the theology of the pericope under
consideration, elucidating what that specific text affirms about God and his
relationship with humankind—the values of the world in front of the text.
And what the pericope so affirms in its theology forms the basis of the
subsequent move to derive application. So biblical interpretation for
application that does not elucidate this crucial intermediary, pericopal
theology, is de facto incomplete, for without discerning this entity by
pragmatic inference from the text, valid application can never be arrived at.
I propose, therefore, a theological exegesis that privileges the text, a
directed exploration that discerns and synthesizes textual clues to arrive at
pericopal theology. Every pericope of Scripture bears literary and stylistic
traces of authors’ agendas, evidence pointing to the authors’ doings, signs
that lead to the discovery of pericopal theology.28 This theological exegesis
is exegesis done expressly in order to arrive at the theology of the pericope,
for only from this intermediary can valid application be discerned.
In sum, in this conception there is a twofold aspect to the sermonic
transaction: the exposition of the theology of the pericope (that is, the move

--- Page 61 ---
from text to theology), and the discovery of how the latter may be applied
in real life (that is, the move from theology to application).
As distinguished from pericopal theology, the operation of biblical
theology tends to be more general, for it develops broad biblical themes
across the canon, with a strong emphasis on timelines and progressive
revelation.29 Invariably, then, the preacher will find that several pericopes,
especially adjoining ones, deal with the same general themes of biblical
theology, potentially resulting in the same sermonic thrust, week after week.
On the other hand, given the degree of specificity and granularity
prescribed by pericopal theology, the particular theological thrust of each
pericope would be heard distinctly, without the weekly repetition of the
broad themes of biblical theology, and making for discrete sermons and
distinct applications weekly. Seeing a text in the wider historical context of
the canon, for which biblical theology is certainly helpful, is not the same as
seeing how a particular pericope makes a specific call to the reader as it
projects a discrete segment of the ideal world of God. It is these specific
pericopal theologies that are essential for the week-by-week life-changing
transactions of preaching that happen in the context of the worship of the
people of God.30 Pericopal theology, thus, helps bring that specific portion
of the biblical text to bear upon the situation of the hearers, thereby aligning
congregation to canon, and God’s people to God’s will in God’s Word by
God’s power.
Applicational Rationale
So how does all this, particularly with regard to preaching Old Testament
pericopes, fit God’s grand scheme of consummating all things in Christ
(Eph. 1:10–11)? Every Christian has an innate sense of the christological
nature of Scripture. The diversity of opinion has been on how exactly
Scripture functions christologically. I propose a way of interpreting texts

--- Page 62 ---
that both respects the fine details of pericopes and also reads them
christologically—what I call a christiconic interpretation.
Christiconic Interpretation
Preaching is God’s gracious call to his people to live with him in his ideal
world, abiding by its values. Since only one Man, the Lord Jesus Christ,
perfectly met every aspect of God’s will, being without sin (2 Cor. 5:21;
Heb. 4:15; 7:26), one can say that this Person, and this Person alone, has
perfectly inhabited the world in front of the text. That is, Jesus Christ alone
has comprehensively abided by the theology of every pericope of Scripture.
Thus, each pericope of the Bible is actually portraying a characteristic of
Christ (a facet of Christ’s image), showing us what it means to perfectly
fulfill, as he did, the particular call of that pericope. The Bible as a whole,
the collection of all its pericopes, then, portrays what a perfect human looks
like, exemplified by Jesus Christ, God incarnate, the perfect Man: the
plenary image of Christ. The written word of God, in this way, depicts the
incarnate Word of God. Scripture is christological!
Thus, sermon by sermon, as God’s people are aligned to the image of
Christ displayed in each pericope, they become progressively more
Christlike. Preaching, therefore, facilitates the conformation of the children
of God into the image of the Son of God by the power of God. After all,

--- Page 63 ---
that is God’s ultimate goal for his children, to be “conformed to the image
[εἰκών, eikōn] of his Son” in his humanity (Rom. 8:29). I have, therefore,
labeled this model of reading Scripture for preaching christiconic.31 Indeed,
“God has destined all his children to the end that they be conformed to
Christ.”32 Paul declared, “We proclaim Him, instructing all people and
teaching all people with all wisdom, that we may present all people mature
in Christ” (Col. 1:28). That, I submit, is the primary function of God’s Word
and, therefore, the primary purpose of preaching, for the Bible is God’s
means to conform his people into the image of his Son, Jesus Christ (by the
power of his Spirit—see below).
In accomplishing this conformation to the image of Christ, God is
restoring the imago Dei, the image of God, in humanity. That image was
humanity’s at creation but was defaced in the fall (Gen. 1:26–27; 9:6; Matt.
22:20 and parallels; 1 Cor. 11:7; James 3:9). But one day, in the plan of
God, humankind will “share his holiness” (Heb. 12:10), fully conformed to
the image of God (in Christ) again, partaking of the divine nature (2 Pet.
1:4; 1 John 3:1–2). Therefore, as far as practical sanctification is concerned
—that is, the impartation of the image of Christ to the believer—the New
Testament consistently points to Jesus as an example: Matthew 10:38;
11:29; 20:26–28; Mark 10:42–45; Luke 22:24–27; John 13:12–17, 34–35;
15:12–13; Romans 6:10–11; 15:2–3, 7; 1 Corinthians 11:1; Ephesians 5:2,
25, 29; Philippians 2:4–11; Colossians 3:13; 1 Thessalonians 1:6; Hebrews
12:3; 1 Peter 2:18–24; 4:1; 1 John 2:5–6; 3:3, 16.33 Luther agreed that
Christ must be imitated as an example, but the mimetic works so performed
with Christ as example must follow the reception of Christ as a gift: “Now
when you have Christ as the foundation and chief blessing of your
salvation, then the other part follows: that you take him as your example.”34
Calvin would heartily concur: “Christ, through whom we return into favor
with God, has been set before us as an example, whose pattern we ought to
express in our life. . . . What can you require beyond this one thing? For we
have been adopted as sons by the Lord with this one condition: that our life
express Christ.”35 Indeed, as C. S. Lewis affirmed, “Every Christian is to
become a little Christ. The whole purpose of becoming a Christian is simply
nothing else.”36 Vanhoozer explains this development of Christlikeness:
“For those who by faith through the Spirit have been united to Christ,
putting on Christ is not a fiction (what if) but a reality (what is). . . .
Disciples do not act like Christ in order to approximate an exemplar outside

--- Page 64 ---
them. Rather, disciples put on Christ from the inside out.” 37 Practical
sanctification is a gradual becoming of what one actually is positionally; it
is a doing congruent to one’s being. That is, the human responsibility to
become Christlike is not a dissembling, but a resembling—a manifestation
of the life of the Lord Jesus Christ already indwelling the Christian; not a
fictional imitation, but a factual actualization, by the power of the Spirit.
I liken preaching, then, to hypothetical multiple, weekly visits to a doctor.
Say you are visiting me, a dermatologist (my other job), this week. I might
tell you how to take care of your dry skin. Next week, if you return, I might
advise you on how to take precautions in the sun. The week after that, you
might be given recommendations regarding your moles. After that, I’d offer
tips on how to care for your hair. Then, your nails. And so on. As you
follow my recommendations, your dermatological status is being improved,
week by week, and you are well on your way to developing perfect skin!
After several weeks of this, you might decide to visit your cardiologist. The
first week she might tell you about controlling your blood pressure. The
week after that, how to maintain an exercise regimen. Then, how to control
your cholesterol with diet and a prescribed statin. And so on, week by
week, till you attain to a perfect cardiovascular state. You might then move
on to an endocrinologist, and onward to a gastroenterologist, and later, a
nephrologist, and so on, slowly and steadily being perfected in health.
So also for preaching. Week by week, pericope by pericope, sermon by
sermon, as we are aligned to God’s specific will in that particular pericope,
to the precepts, priorities, and practices of that pericopal segment of the
world in front of the text (i.e., pericopal theology), we are being
increasingly molded into the image of Christ, the only one who fully kept
God’s will, and who alone perfectly inhabits the world in front of the text.
In summary, the incarnated Word, the Lord Jesus Christ, portrayed in the
inscripturated Word, is the summit of revelation in whose image humanity
is to be conformed, in the power of God’s Spirit. Therefore, preaching
Christ in this sense is the proper goal of preaching, for it is the will of God
to conform his people to that image of his Son: christiconic interpretation.
Preaching Is Trinitarian
And with a christiconic hermeneutic, preaching also becomes trinitarian
in concept and function. Regarding the three entities that ground preaching

--- Page 65 ---
—text, pericopal theology, and application—we see that each is related to a
specific Person of the Trinity. The text inspired by the Holy Spirit (2 Pet.
1:21) depicts Jesus Christ, the Son, to whose image humans are to conform
(Rom. 8:29). As they are so conformed, the will of God the Father is being
done and his kingdom is coming to pass (Matt. 6:10).
In other words, the agenda of God the Spirit in Scripture reveals the
perfect, divine will-fulfilling life of God the Son, so that when it is applied
by believers, the kingdom of God the Father is being established as his
children are aligned to his will.38 Thus, preaching is not merely for the
information of minds, but for the transformation of lives—that they may be
conformed to the image of Christ, in the power of the Holy Spirit, through
the instrumentality of Scripture, by the agency of the preacher. Week by
week, sermon by sermon, pericope by pericope, habits are changed,
dispositions are created, character is built, the image of Christ is formed,
and humans are becoming what they were meant by God to be.39
Homiletical Rationale
In this section, I will touch on a couple of practical, but critical, aspects of
this entire discussion. How does the responsibility of the children of God to
apply Scripture interface with their relationship to their heavenly Father?
And, related to that, what is the role of God in this process of practical
sanctification and conformation to Christlikeness?
Relationship Precedes Responsibility
Preaching for alignment with divine will in the text is not justification
oriented; that is, obedience in application can never earn merit toward

--- Page 66 ---
salvation. Rather, it is sanctification oriented, intended for those already in
relationship to God. And, in Scripture, relationship to God is always
followed by responsibility.40 That is, when men and women come into
relationship with God, God always gives guidance as to how they should
then live—that is, in accordance with the precepts, priorities, and practices
of his ideal world, his kingdom, his will. Indeed, such a theme resonates
through the Pentateuch. God elected a people; then he required of them
obedience to his will. It is notable that the Ten Commandments
(responsibility) were prefaced by an announcement of relationship: “I am
Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the
house of slavery” (Exod. 20:2). Relationship always precedes responsibility.
Yahweh’s being the Israelites’ God—a relationship inaugurated prior to the
giving of the Mosaic law—had significant bearing upon the people’s
subsequent responsibility to be holy. Yahweh had separated his people to
himself as his possession; therefore, they were to be as holy as he was.41
“First God redeems Israel from Egypt, and then he gives the law, so
obedience to the law is a response to God’s grace, not an attempt to gain
righteousness by works.”42 In other words, obedience is the response of
God’s people to his prevenient operations of grace: relationship precedes
responsibility. The initiation of the divine-human relationship was entirely a
unilateral divine act of love and grace, one that was (and always is)
apprehended from the human side by faith alone.43 Thus, God’s plan, all
along, has been to direct the behavior of those who were already his
children. A relationship with God always precedes the responsibility of the
people of God to accede to the will of God to be as holy as God.44
Therefore, a loving relationship with God should result in the keeping of
his commandments, as the New Testament is not hesitant to point out,
particularly in Johannine literature: John 14:15, 21; 15:10, 12; 1 John 2:3–5;
3:22–24; 4:21; 5:2–3; and 2 John 6. And it is the role of each pericope of
Scripture to portray what the will of God is (the theology of the pericope,
what happens in God’s ideal world, in his kingdom), so that we, God’s
children, might be aligned to it in the power of the Spirit to become holy, as
God, our Father, is holy.45 Pericopal theology thus provides the text’s
direction for holiness, and the preacher’s task is to help God’s people
discern and apply this theology into the concrete circumstances of their
lives. Their obedience would then be the discharging of their responsibility

--- Page 67 ---
to reflect their relationship with God. And through this obedience, God is
glorified as his people manifest his holiness and image Christ to the world.
What Christ Hath Wrought
This discussion of practical, ongoing sanctification and alignment to
Christ’s image, and of the responsibility of God’s people to apply Scripture
(thus moving closer to Christlikeness), should not be misconstrued as a sort
of do-it-yourself lifting up of oneself by one’s own theological bootstraps.46
Not at all! The gradual conformation to the image of Christ in this life (and
ultimate conformation in the next) is a matter of God’s grace—
notwithstanding the component of human responsibility to obey divine will.
Here I echo Wright:
Everything . . . about moral effort, about the conscious shaping of our patterns of behavior, takes
place simply and solely within the framework of grace—the grace which was embodied in Jesus
and his death and resurrection, the grace which is active in the Spirit-filled preaching of the
gospel, the grace which continues to be active by the Spirit in the lives of believers. It is simply
not the case that God does some of the work of our salvation and we have to do the rest. It is not
the case that we begin by being justified by grace through faith and then have to go to work all
by ourselves to complete the job by struggling, unaided, to live a holy life.47
Without the grace extended to humanity in Christ, the law of God in every
age is shown up for its incapacity: in and of itself law can do nothing to
justify the sinner, it has no expiatory provision to wipe away sin, it
exercises no forgiving grace to effect reconciliation with God, and it cannot
empower obedience to its own requirements. All law does is depict God’s
holy standard and pronounce sentence upon those who contravene that
standard. But with Christ, all things have been made anew: sinners have
been justified, sin expiated, forgiveness gained, and reconciliation with God
accomplished for those who believe in Jesus Christ as their only God and
Savior. And it is not just his atonement that is efficacious for believers’
sanctification, but also his ongoing ministry before the Father for the ones
he redeemed: his intercession for them (Rom. 8:34; Heb. 7:25) and his
advocacy on their behalf (Heb. 9:24; 1 John 2:1).
But this newness of creation that the Christian is does not nullify the call
of God in each pericope of Scripture: relationship with God mandates
responsibility toward God. So there is yet another aspect of the work of
Christ that is crucial to the Christian’s sanctification: Christ’s sending of the
Holy Spirit (John 14:16; 15:26; 16:7) and Christ’s baptism of believers in

--- Page 68 ---
that divine Spirit, uniting them to himself (Mark 1:8; Rom. 6:3–14; 1 Cor.
12:13; Gal. 3:27). And with this indwelling of the Holy Spirit in believers, a
new life is begun, and they are enabled to fulfill the call of God in all of
Scripture. Indeed, Christ’s work was accomplished so that God’s will (his
“righteous requirement”) may be fulfilled by his Spirit-indwelt and Spirit-
empowered children, “who walk not according to the flesh but according to
the Spirit” (Rom. 8:4, 12–16). That it is the Holy Spirit who gives one the
ability to obey God is also established by the Old Testament: see, for
example, Deuteronomy 30:6; Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–28; 37:1–
28.48 All of Scripture attests to the working of God through the Spirit in and
with the work of his children (John 15:5; Eph. 2:10; 3:16; Phil. 2:13; 4:13;
Col. 1:9–11), enabling a life of obedience that pleases him (Heb. 13:20–21;
also Phil. 2:12–13; Heb. 13:16; 1 John 3:22).49
All that to say, it is all of grace, yet there is Christian responsibility. How
practical sanctification unto Christlikeness is both a function of divine
sovereignty and human responsibility is an inscrutable question. “We are
here, as so often in theology, at the borders of language, because we are
trying to talk at the same time about ‘something God does’ and ‘something
humans do’ as if God were simply another character like ourselves, as
though (in other words) the interplay of God’s work and our work could be
imagined on the model of two people collaborating on a project. There are
mysteries here.”50
In sum, the child of God is never to attempt obedience with self-
resources: that would be a self-glorifying, flesh-driven, merit-attempting,
grace-rejecting, faith-negating obedience to divine law—the legalism Paul
so often excoriated. Utterly futile. Instead, the “obedience of faith” (Rom.
1:5; 16:26) that God empowers is a God-glorifying, Spirit-driven, merit-
rejecting, grace-accepting, faith-exercising endeavor.51 So, Christian life, in
its entirety, is a function of divine grace, designed to bring glory to God: the
Father’s choice of men and women to become a holy people in Christ
(justification), their empowerment by the Spirit to live lives that are
Christlike (sanctification), and, one day, consummation of their
transformation into the image of Jesus Christ (glorification).
And so with the author of Hebrews we can affirm that God is the one
equipping his children to do his will, even as God himself does it all
(13:20–21)—that is how God is pleased and glorified: “Now the God of
peace, who brought up from the dead the great Shepherd of the sheep . . .

--- Page 69 ---
even Jesus our Lord, equip you in every good thing to do [ποιέω, poieō:
human doing] his will, he doing [ποιέω: divine doing] in us that which is
pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever and
ever. Amen!”
Conclusion
As C. S. Lewis remarked: “The first qualification for judging any piece of
workmanship from a corkscrew to a cathedral is to know what it is—what it
was intended to do and how it is meant to be used.”52 I submit that
Scripture was primarily intended to be used for application, so that God’s
people, aligned to the Father’s will, might be conformed to the image
(εἰκών, eikōn) of God’s Son in his perfect humanity, by the power of God’s
Spirit: a christiconic goal (Rom. 8:29). Therefore, we must read and
interpret Scripture for application unto Christlikeness. William Tyndale’s
words are apropos:
Though we read Scripture, and never stop babbling about it, if we know not its use, and why it
was given, and what should be sought in it, it profits us nothing at all. It is not enough,
therefore, to read and talk of it only, but we must also beseech God insistently, day and night, to
make us understand why the Scriptures were given, that we may apply the medicine of
Scripture, every man to his own wounds. If not, we remain idle disputers, and brawlers about
vain words, ever gnawing upon the bitter bark outside, and never reaching the sweet pith
inside.53
This is the heart of the preaching endeavor: to recognize the function of
Scripture and to bring to bear, pericope by pericope, divine guidelines for
life from the Word of God upon the people of God, to align them to the will
of God by the power of the Spirit of God, into the image of the Son of God
for the glory of God. Through believers’ Spirit-powered obedience,
facilitated by preaching, God is glorified as his people display the attributes
of their holy God and manifest Christlikeness (Matt. 5:16; 1 Pet. 2:12): a
christiconic undertaking. The call of preachers to facilitate such a God-
glorifying transaction is, therefore, a marvelous one—a doxological task, as
God’s kingdom is being brought about. “To him [be] the glory in the church
and in Christ Jesus to all generations, forever and ever. Amen” (Eph. 3:21).

Response to Abraham Kuruvilla
BRYAN CHAPELL
When I read Abe Kuruvilla, I always rejoice for the gift his work is to the
church of Jesus Christ. Not only does Kuruvilla provide some of the finest
scholarship available in contemporary homiletics; he also brings a
commitment to the truth and integrity of Scripture that is sorely needed in a
relativistic age. I admire his close reading of biblical texts and rejoice that
he is familiar with the contributions of secular communication theory but
not seduced by its subtleties.
When I read the totality of his arguments, I find them beneficial and often
undoing the preconceptions I have about the thrust of his approach. I do not
think that what I will offer in following paragraphs will expose any
foundational differences in our approaches, but will only suggest where I
would appreciate emphases that would safeguard his intentions and mine.
The intention of his biblical argument is “to privilege the text and its
immediate context to figure out what the author was doing with what he
was saying in this text” (50). That is surely a correct goal, but I would want
it to be pursued with definitions of key terms developed more thoroughly
than Kuruvilla’s argument allows but that he actually demonstrates in
practice.
For example, the discussion of the Abraham narrative that Kuruvilla so
brilliantly dissects ends with the summary, “Now Abraham so loved God
that he gave his only begotten son” (50). Then, Kuruvilla follows with the
advice, “We must focus upon what the author is doing with what he is
saying in that particular text in order to elicit valid application for readers”
(50–51). The question I have is, Which author (Author)?
There is a canonical Author at work at the same time there is a human
author at work. I do not doubt that Moses did not perceive all the
implications of God’s promise to provide a sacrifice for Abraham’s only
begotten son, but when Kuruvilla borrows the words of John 3:16 to explain
the purpose of the narrative, I think he is doing precisely the right thing. He

--- Page 75 ---
is showing how there is a grace on display that will culminate in a greater
work of grace being signaled by the canonical Author.
The close reading of the text provided by Kuruvilla brings into stark
relief the grace of a God who provides what his people cannot provide for
themselves—and every biblical theologian should privilege the text this
way. The later claim in Kuruvilla’s essay that, “as distinguished from
pericopal theology, the operation of biblical theology tends to be more
general, for it develops broad biblical themes across the canon” (58) creates
an unnecessary either/or. I know of no credible biblical theologian who
thinks it is unnecessary to examine each pericope in detail. We simply insist
that it is necessary to examine context (immediate and canonical) as well as
text—to operate synthetically as well as atomistically—in order to
determine what the Author is doing with what he inspires his authors to say.
I heartily agree with Kuruvilla’s theological rationale that seeks to
determine the purpose (significance) of the text without settling for bare
meaning or fact recitation. The idea that the “world in front of the text”
identifies “God’s gracious invitation to humankind to live in his ideal world
by abiding by the thrust of that pericope” (56) is a rich and helpful concept.
I struggle, however, with the conclusion that follows: “As we are
captivated by the vision of God’s ideal world, the various aspects of
Christian life, individual and corporate, are gradually—one pericope at a
time—being brought into alignment with the will of God” (56). Yes, that is
part of the story. But the other part of the story is, as Kuruvilla states, that
“Jesus Christ alone has comprehensively abided by the theology of every
pericope of Scripture” (59).
Thus, to preach the pericopal application of any text apart from
identification of the accompanying, inherent, and necessary grace of God
automatically condemns human listeners to failure and judgment against
Jesus’s standard. This cannot be the entirety of the good news we are to
proclaim. Kuruvilla knows this and, as the fine gospel preacher he is,
addresses the issue in his homiletical rationale, saying, “Relationship
always precedes responsibility” (64). He adds, “[Ongoing sanctification] is
all of grace, yet there is Christian responsibility” (68).
There should be no argument with those applicational conclusions. The
matter most biblical theologians would press at this point is that the
preacher needs to make plain the features of the text proving that
relationship precedes responsibility and disclosing the grace that enables it.

--- Page 76 ---
If the sum of a pericopal application is simply, “Shape up and be like
Jesus,” then false pride or honest despair is the only possible human
response. Again, as Kuruvilla notes, only Jesus meets that standard.
This does not mean that there is no application to be made, but rather that
the call to human responsibility is equally a call to reliance upon the grace
of God. As Kuruvilla says, “And so with the author of Hebrews we can
affirm that God is the one equipping his children to do his will, even as God
himself does it all” (68).
Thus, the application of Scripture is not simply “application unto
Christlikeness” (69); it is also application by Christ’s enabling. As
Kuruvilla says, “The child of God is never to attempt obedience with self-
resources: that would be a self-glorifying, flesh-driven, merit-attempting,
grace-rejecting, faith-negating obedience to divine law” (68). Amen.
Advocates of biblical theology would urge preachers not merely to think
this, but to say it—and, more importantly, to show how the text says it.
If the text is carefully examined not only for its behavioral instruction but
also for its revelation of how God is providing for people who cannot
provide all that he requires (or they need), then the grace of God will shine
in every context. Discerning such grace is not the same as trying to make
Jesus magically appear in every pericope. That would be futile and wrong.
Rather, such discernment examines each text’s details and context to
disclose our human nature that requires the grace of God’s instruction and
enabling, as well as to disclose the character of the God who provides such
grace, which culminates in the person and work of Christ.

Response to Abraham Kuruvilla
KENNETH LANGLEY
Abe Kuruvilla has enriched church and academy in recent years with
impressive work on hermeneutics and homiletics. He reads widely, thinks
deeply, and writes clearly. Readers who are encountering him for the first
time in this volume will want to check out his commentaries, blog
(www.homiletix.com), and books on preaching.
I appreciate his attention to the details of the text, which (it should go
without saying) “produces tremendous yield for preaching” (50). I applaud
his emphasis on pragmatics, what human authors and divine Author are
doing with what they are saying. I share his concern that privileging
systematic or biblical theology in the interpretation of the Bible’s numerous
and varied pericopes results in unfortunate sameness in the pulpit week
after week. I agree with him that valid application is much more difficult
than many realize (and I believe that preachers who learn from this wise
tutor will negotiate the move from text to application with greater fidelity to
God’s intentions). I could add much more by way of affirmation, but would
only repeat what Kuruvilla has said and said better. My assignment now is
to raise points of disagreement; these are few and unimportant compared to
my enthusiasm for his body of work.
Kuruvilla advocates a crucial move between text and application, which
he describes, first, as inferring a segment of the world in front of the text;
second, as discerning the theology of the pericope; and third, as seeing a
facet of the image of Christ. I’m sold on the validity of the second,
discerning the pericopal theology. The first and third, not so much.
I wonder if projecting a world in front of the text is the most helpful way
to construe what authors are doing, and whether interpreters must, in fact,
discern this ideal world before they can faithfully exposit a passage.
Granted, preachers need to know something of pragmatics. And Kuruvilla’s
illustration of standing on someone else’s foot makes that point simply and
convincingly without introducing the somewhat difficult notion of a
projected world. Granted, a theological “intermediary” between text and

--- Page 78 ---
application is needed. I think “pericopal theology” fits the bill nicely.
Granted, the preacher must ask, What is the author doing in this text and
what does God want to do in the sermon on this text? But I think this
already challenging inquiry becomes more complicated—unnecessarily
complicated—when one speaks of “projecting” and “inhabiting”
“segments” of an ideal world, or even by speaking of an “ideal world”
when the world the preacher and listeners will literally inhabit after the
sermon is still very much not ideal this side of the eschaton. I’ve used
illustrations similar to Kuruvilla’s “You’re on my foot” and “the trash is
full” to help beginning preachers grasp pragmatics. I want them to see, for
example, that we preach the Beatitudes differently when we know that Jesus
is not issuing eight new commandments but announcing a different kind of
kingdom, or that we preach some prophetic oracles differently when we
understand that though formally addressed to Israel’s pagan neighbors, they
were meant to be overheard by God’s people and thereby to encourage
them. I have advocated pericopal theology (and heartily recommended
Kuruvilla’s Privilege the Text!), hoping that students will preach from the
theology of their assigned text, not (or not solely) from broader theological
constructs. But I’ve commended this attentiveness to pragmatics without
what seems to me potentially confusing mental exercises in projecting ideal
world segments.
I also have reservations about finding a facet of the image of Christ in
each preaching portion. Could that search be misleading, and is it even
necessary? It may be misleading if what a text asks of us is behavior that
Christ never had to exemplify, namely repentance. It may be misleading if
Christ as example looms larger than Christ as Redeemer, Mediator,
Counselor, and Lord. This is not Kuruvilla’s intention, as he makes clear in
what he says about grace and relationship preceding responsibility. But
naming his chapter “Christiconic View” seems to privilege Christ’s role as
example.
Even if it’s possible to search for the image of Christ in each pericope in
a way that’s not misleading, is this search necessary? Maybe the thrust of
the text’s pericopal theology is not exemplary but, say, doxological. Maybe
the thrust is exemplary, but it’s the Father we are urged to imitate (as in the
Sermon on the Mount). “Behold your God,” the preacher proclaims,
without having to specify one person of the Trinity.

--- Page 79 ---
Kuruvilla’s essay and mine are mostly in agreement. Whereas Chapell
and Wilson champion a broader theological theme that should shape every
sermon, Kuruvilla and I wish to concentrate on the specific theological
claim of the text at hand. I can even summarize my view by using
Kuruvilla’s sentences if I change just a few key words. He writes, “Every
Christian has an innate sense of the christological nature of Scripture” (59),
and he proposes “a way of interpreting texts that both respects the fine
details of pericopes and also reads them christologically—what I call a
christiconic interpretation” (59). I would counter that every Christian has an
innate sense of the theological nature of Scripture, and I propose a way of
interpreting texts that both respects the fine details of pericopes and also
reads them theologically—what I call theocentric preaching.

Response to Abraham Kuruvilla
PAUL SCOTT WILSON
Since the 1950s, homileticians have reclaimed the Word of God as an event.
What this means has varied, but often it is in the sense of Isaiah 55:11: “My
word . . . shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I
purpose” (NRSV). Some scholars conceive of the sermon as a language
event, as in plotted action and language generating an experience of the
Bible. Fred Craddock and Thomas Long take the sermon to be an event of
the text: the text says something and it does something (Long’s focus
statement and function statement).1 From my perspective, it is an event (1)
of God’s action, in or behind the text, in one of the Persons of the Trinity,
and (2) of Christ meeting us in the proclamation of the gospel. Kuruvilla
opens an additional perspective that focuses on the biblical authors, who
both say something (the sentence as code) and intend something in the
world in front of the text (the author’s theological agenda). Something is
happening there that the hearer needs to enter. This world in front of the text
represents the Christlike ideal for which the hearer is to strive. This
approach stresses human action, or what I call law or trouble, over divine
action, giving priority to what in history is known as the moral sense of
Scripture.
Kuruvilla’s theology is filled with grace. He speaks of each pericope
containing “God’s gracious invitation” (56) to live in that projected world;
he says that without the grace extended in Christ, “the law of God in every
age is shown up for its incapacity” to save (66); in the sermon, God
graciously invites the reader into the world in front of the text. He says,
“The child of God is never to attempt obedience with self-resources: that
would be . . . utterly futile” (68). However, Kuruvilla’s guidelines for
preaching provide little help in getting to grace in sermons. What steps
might he devise to help preachers not cast listeners on their own resources?
As opposed to christocentric preaching, he recommends christiconic
preaching—that is, Christ-iconic: every text offers a small part of the
portrait of Christ. When one follows the instructions for living that texts

--- Page 81 ---
provide, week after week, one’s character becomes molded in the image of
Christ and one is guided toward sanctified living. This is a helpful way of
thinking about preaching and sanctification. What of other issues in the
world that believers bring with them to worship, like wars, famines, inner-
city and domestic violence, poverty, racism, and so forth? Should “authorial
intent” be plural, “authorial intentions”? Do the textual author(s) also point,
not least, to revelation of God’s action and character in relation to social
problems?
Kuruvilla employs (and, like Luke in profession, represents) a medical
model for homiletics. One strength of that model is that authority rests with
the doctor, who has the necessary knowledge. On the other hand, that same
model may seem too singular or controlling, for instance, regarding
questions of biblical authorship, the variety of legitimate meanings in a
given text, or a christiconic approach that seems willing to overlook the
gospel. Or his approach may seem too clinical, at times unreceptive to the
pastoral concerns of the people. I like Kuruvilla’s “world in front of the
text.” It addresses a dimension of the text that can be overlooked. It is not
very far in front of the text and may be a part of it. It is not the familiar
“world in front of the text” that Stanley Fish (Is There a Text in This Class?)
and other scholars present, in reader-response criticism. Their “world in
front” consists of experiences readers bring to the text that help determine
what meaning they find. These two “worlds” are both legitimate, but are
vastly different. I think both need to be engaged.
Kuruvilla uses the example of 1 Samuel 15:3 and cites the command to
“kill both man and woman, child and infant” (NRSV) of the Amalekites.
Kuruvilla says the prophet passes on this demand of the Lord, and he draws
our attention to the translation of the word meaning “voice” from the
original Hebrew. He takes us to his “world in front of the text”: “the author
is doing something with this narrative, telling readers that the one
committed to God listens to the voice of God, not the voice of worldly
seductions” (46).
Can one safely ignore that other “world in front of the text” that is
common now in literary theory? The reality here is that the preacher sounds
clinical or emotionally detached. Once the command in the text is uttered,
its impact on the hearers will be immediate and needs to be anticipated. A
teenager who loves violent video games will hear it differently from a
young mother, or a veteran who has witnessed the reality and horror of

--- Page 82 ---
slaughter. Because Kuruvilla’s “world in front of the text” has less to do
with the world the reader actually inhabits than with the world the reader is
meant to inhabit, it is ideal, drawing the best out of the text. But the hearer
is stuck with the worst in the text, the idea that God would command even
women and children to be killed. This other “world in front of the text,” the
context of the readers with all its brokenness, suffering, and sin, is linked
with the fallen world in the text. I suspect that for many hearers to
contemplate or accept the good insights Kuruvilla offers about the text, their
more immediate concerns may first need to be addressed.
I love Kuruvilla’s idea that “application is what the texts of Scripture
were primarily written for” (53). Would his approach be strengthened if
more attention were paid both to what God is doing in and behind texts and
to that other “world in front of the text”? Biblical authorial intent, what the
author is doing, is not singular and needs additional definitions and
guidelines. “Hey, you are standing on my foot!” (54) in fact does not
necessarily project a world where no one ever stands on A’s foot. Perhaps A
encouraged B to stand on the foot, and congratulates B for doing so; or A
wants B to sit on A’s foot, not stand.
Kuruvilla’s “world in front of the text” may not accommodate other
applications that readers bring with them. This is clear in the fascinating
rendering of Genesis 22, building on the subsequent absence of Isaac from
the Abraham stories. What the biblical author is said to be doing, in the
world in front of the text, is stressing that “the love/fear of God is to trump
every other human allegiance” (50). It is a good reading. In the other “world
in front of the text,” where readers reside, even the slaughter of an animal
can be troubling for some, and child abuse and infanticide are painful, daily,
tragic news. Preachers need to step into that world to avoid seeming
clinical. How would someone who has been abused hear this text? Faithful
believers might legitimately wonder if the reason father and son are
thereafter separated is not the theological point Kuruvilla intends, but the
possibility that the son could never again trust the father, no matter what the
divine outcome. The hearers need to know that the preacher can be a pastor
to them. Application needs to take account of, and reconcile, both worlds in
front of the text.

Theocentric View
KENNETH LANGLEY
Biblical Rationale
Preaching should be God centered because God is God centered and wants
us to be God centered in everything we do. All God does he does for his
glory, and all we do—eating, drinking, and certainly preaching—we do for
his glory (1 Cor. 10:31).1 Preachers may take up a variety of texts and
topics, but they should take them up (and their hearers with them) all the
way into the presence of God, so that listeners are instructed by the Word of
God, convinced of the value of God, captivated by the holiness, grace,
kingship, wisdom, and beauty of God. Preaching is all about and all for
God.2
The Bible’s own preaching is manifestly God centered, as two
paradigmatic texts illustrate, one from each Testament.3
Isaiah 40:9 and the Preaching of the Old Testament
Isaiah charges his fellow preachers: “You who bring good news to Zion,
go up on a high mountain. You who bring good news to Jerusalem, lift up
your voice with a shout, lift it up, do not be afraid; say to the towns of
Judah, ‘Here is your God!’” (40:9).
What does Zion need to hear? “Behold your God!” What message do
gospel heralds still shout from the mountaintops? “Look at God!” What is
the burden of preachers today, centuries after Isaiah? “Take your eyes off
idols and empire and self, and gaze at God!” Everything the prophets said,
everything God himself said through them, was meant to render God central
in the minds and hearts of listeners. Think of the refrain in Ezekiel,
following promises, warnings, doom oracles, and taunts: “Then [you/they]

--- Page 85 ---
will know that I am the LORD” (Ezek. 36:23, for example; the refrain occurs
sixty-five times in the book).
The prophets did not make predictions to satisfy the curiosity of readers
centuries later; they spoke of the future of God. They did not scold or nag;
they reminded listeners of God’s law and covenant. They did not speak of
an abstraction called social justice; they pressed the just claims of God on
people and nations. “In prophetic literature, the theocentric emphasis is so
evident that it is hard to ignore.”4
This theocentric emphasis is not more narrowly christocentric. Though
some Old Testament prophecies anticipate the Messiah, the effort of some
preachers to find the second person of the Trinity in every prophetic
pericope is misguided. Peter said that all the prophets testified about Christ
(Acts 10:43; cf. 1 Pet. 1:21); but it’s one thing to say the prophets point to
Christ, another to say every word they wrote is about Christ.5 One
theological commentary claims, “The book of Jonah is all about Christ.”
No, the book of Jonah is all about God.6
What was true of the writing prophets was true of the “former prophets,”
what we typically refer to as the historical books. The material here is not
sermonic, but it “preaches” in the sense that its interests go beyond mere
history or storytelling to disclosing God and God’s will for the world. These
Old Testament narratives relate how God has acted, in, through, and despite
the actions of the human characters. The agenda is theological. To
concentrate on human deeds, then, is often to miss the point.7 (However,
recognizing that God is central in these stories does not mean they have no
exemplar value for moral instruction: see comments below in the section on
applicational rationale.) Sometimes the theocentric focus is sharpened by
explicit editorial comments (2 Sam. 11:27; 2 Kings 17:7, 18); sometimes
God is more noticeable for the narrator’s not mentioning him (such as in the
book of Esther, and in a different way, Ruth). But everywhere, Old
Testament history is his story.
Dale Ralph Davis offers practical counsel on theocentric preaching of
Old Testament narratives: keep the great theological themes in mind while
studying a passage, invite listeners to get on board with the action of God in
the text, and make God the subject of the sermon’s sentences. These
practices foster “a discipline of always looking at our texts from a
theocentric view.”8

--- Page 86 ---
There can be no doubt about the theocentric nature of Old Testament
legal material. The law is God’s law, given to God’s people that they might
know and love and please God (Deut. 6:1–9). Even commandments that are
not self-evidently “religious,” like those regulating sexual conduct,
employment, or court proceedings, are punctuated by “I am the LORD” (Lev.
18; 19), reminding Israel that these are covenant stipulations and that
covenant precedes commandment (Exod. 19; 20). Ethical behavior is
defined by the character of God and flows from a relationship with God.
How Mosaic law applies to Christians is a question for another day, but it
certainly helps the preacher who is trying to be faithful to this part of
Scripture to focus on what it tells us about our Creator-Redeemer.9
In Psalms, the people of God live under the good hand of God, praising
God and complaining to God, claiming the promises of God, treasuring the
law of God, affirming the mission of God, celebrating the power and
creativity and steadfast love of God. Laments struggle with the apparent
absence of God and the puzzling silence of God. Messianic psalms
anticipate the coming of God’s ideal King. Although most psalms are
speech addressed to God, their inclusion in the canon and use by the New
Testament testifies that they are also from God. In these prayers and songs
God speaks and God is revealed. What J. I. Packer said of the rest of
Scripture applies to the Psalter: “The Bible is God preaching.”10
The Wisdom literature of the Old Testament also preaches, and preaches
a theocentric message. Though its subject matter is less concerned with cult
and worship than some other genres, and though we do not hear in it a
direct “Thus says the LORD,” this literature reminds us that the fear of the
LORD is the starting point and chief component of wisdom (Prov. 1:7).
We’re assured that life is not, after all, meaningless, that everything matters
when life is lived coram deo (Eccles. 12:13–14). Job knows that God alone
possesses wisdom, wisdom we can share in some measure when God grants
it to us (Job 28:23, 28). Even in Song of Solomon we can find God’s subtle
presence.11
In preaching both law and Wisdom literature we may tend to state the
obvious and earthbound: how people fall short, how life under the sun is
hebel (vanity). So Elizabeth Achtemeier reminds us that a theocentric
approach is required: “It is very easy for anybody to tell us what is wrong
with our world and what are the common struggles and sins of us human
beings. We have only to look about us or to read the morning headlines.

--- Page 87 ---
Even a twelve-year-old can compile a list of the evils in our society. But it
takes a preacher to say what God is doing about them.”12 What does the
New Testament say about God-centeredness?
Mark 1:14 and the Preaching of the New Testament
Mark says that our Lord began his public ministry “proclaiming the good
news of God” (Mark 1:14). Jesus’s preaching was concerned with the reign
of God (1:15), the will of God (3:34–35), the “concerns” of God (8:33),
faith in God (11:22), and the love of God (12:30). His parables were about
the kingdom of God.
Though the phrase “the good news of God” does not appear in Matthew
or Luke, these evangelists, too, witness to the God-centeredness of our
Lord’s preaching. Matthew’s summary of Jesus’s teaching in the Sermon on
the Mount is so theocentric, so Father-centric, it can be described in the
words J. I. Packer used for the rest of the New Testament: “You sum up the
whole of New Testament teaching in a single phrase if you speak of it as a
revelation of the Fatherhood of the holy Creator.”13 Though Jesus preached
with unprecedented personal authority (Matt. 7:28–29), he confessed, “All
things have been committed to me by my Father” (Matt. 11:27; Luke
10:22). As God the Son incarnate, he was uniquely qualified to reveal
spiritual truth in his preaching; what he revealed was, preeminently, the
Father (Matt. 11:27).
Luke’s Jesus taught that those who are rightly related to him are those
and only those who listen to God and obey him (Luke 8:21; 11:27–28). He
commissioned his followers to preach the kingdom of God (9:2, 60; 10:9)
and did so himself. The kingdom of God was unquestionably the dominant
theme of his proclamation (4:43). In this gospel we find two dominical
sayings often cited in support of Christ-centered preaching, Luke 24:27 and
24:44. But in these postresurrection scenes, as in John 5:39, Jesus says that
all parts of Scripture (law, prophets, and writings) testify of him; he does
not say that every line of every Old Testament pericope is about him.
The Fourth Gospel is, if anything, more theocentric than the Synoptics.
Everything Jesus says and does he says and does to glorify the Father (John
5:19; 10:37; 12:28, 49–50; 14:13). He comes from the Father (1:14), returns
to the Father (20:17), reveals the Father (1:18), obeys the Father (5:36), and
speaks only what the Father tells him (8:38). John’s Christology is arguably

--- Page 88 ---
the “highest” of the four Gospels, but no one is clearer than John that the
Son is subject to the Father and lives to make him known. If this is the
burden of our Lord himself and the evangelists who told his story, should it
not be ours as well?
When it comes to the rest of the New Testament, Bultmann thought “the
proclaimer became the proclaimed,” which Fred Craddock paraphrases,
“Jesus came preaching God but the early church preached Jesus Christ.”14
Not so, says Craddock. Not so, says Walter Brueggemann: though the
narrative revolves around Christ, the kerygma “clearly assumes the action
of the God of Israel who is the God of the church.”15 In Acts and the
epistles, it is still God who creates, calls, redeems, sanctifies, guides, gives,
commands, empowers, and promises. It is God who sent Christ and God
who will send Christ again at the end of history. Robert Brawley pays close
attention to the verbs in Luke-Acts and concludes that God is the main
actor; the narrative is thoroughly theocentric.16
Hebrews, the closest thing we have to a complete sermon in the Bible,
begins and ends with God: God spoke through his Son (1:1–2) and God
blesses and equips his people (13:20–25). In between these bracketing
statements, God’s Son is exalted; he is seen to be greater than Moses,
Melchizedek, temple, and angels. Even so, it is through Christ that we
know, praise, and approach God, and it is through Christ that God equips
his saints (1:2; 7:25; 10:20; 13:15, 21).
James preached a message so God centered, with so little about Christ
and justification, Luther did not care for it. Peter’s first epistle is to “God’s
elect” (1 Pet. 1:1); it’s all about God choosing (1:2), regenerating (1:3), and
shielding (1:5) God’s people (2:10). Why did Christ die, according to this
epistle? “To bring you to God” (3:18). John Piper unpacks this verse,
showing that justification and forgiveness and peace and all that comes to
those who trust God’s appointed Savior are wonderful gospel benefits, but
God himself is the gospel. “God is the gospel.” What makes the gospel
good news is that in Christ God has removed all obstacles to our glorifying
and enjoying him forever.17
The last book of the Bible is “the revelation of Jesus Christ” in the sense
that God gave it to Jesus to show to his servants (Rev. 1:1); the content of
Revelation is manifestly about God from start to finish. The appearing of
our Lord Jesus Christ is something “God will bring about in his own time”
(1 Tim. 6:15).

--- Page 89 ---
Even Paul, whose life was so thoroughly revolutionized when he was
apprehended by Christ, nonetheless casts a broader theological vision than
Jesus only. He sees redemptive history moving from the creation of the
world, through which God’s “eternal power and divine nature” are clearly
seen (Rom. 1:20), toward a consummation in which the Son hands over the
kingdom to his Father, and the Son himself is “subject to him who put
everything under him, so that God may be all in all” (1 Cor. 15:24, 28). The
drama of the Bible, for Paul, begins and ends with God.
True, Paul resolved to know nothing among the Corinthians but “Jesus
Christ and him crucified” (1 Cor. 2:2). But this message, he says in the
preceding sentence, was a “testimony about God.” It’s not surprising that
Paul, writing to a proud congregation enamored of worldly wisdom and
rhetoric, would remind them that his humble message featured a crucified
Messiah. But surely in the hundreds of hours Paul ministered in Corinth he
talked about more than the cross. From his sermons in Acts we know that
the resurrection, at least, got some air time! In Acts 17 and 2 Timothy 2:8
he was able to summarize his gospel without even mentioning the cross.
The God-centeredness of the Bible’s own preaching and that of Jesus
himself is reason enough to be God centered in preaching. Some will say
preaching must move beyond theocentric to christocentric. But why should
we think it insufficient or sub-Christian to say, like the prophets, “Behold
your God!” (Isa. 40:9), or like the apostles to be “set apart for the gospel of
God” (Rom. 1:1), or like Jesus to proclaim “the good news of God” (Mark
1:14)? What could be more Christ exalting than to imitate Christ’s own
thoroughgoing God-centeredness?
Sidney Greidanus speaks for many christocentric homileticians:
preachers should “look for a road from the periphery to the center of the
Bible and redemptive history—a road from their text to Jesus Christ.”18 I
second the motion that preaching should be theological as well as textual,
and that sermons should move toward the “center.” But I would change the
name at the end of Greidanus’s sentence to “God.” God is at the center of
the Bible. God is at the heart of redemptive history. He is its main character.
His Son “gave himself for our sins to rescue us from the present evil age,
according to the will of our God and Father, to whom be glory for ever and
ever. Amen” (Gal. 1:4–5).

Theological Rationale
“The goal of preaching is the glory of God in the glad submission of his
people.”19 Where did John Piper get that idea? From Jonathan Edwards,
who got it from the Bible. Edwards carefully demonstrated that God’s
purpose in everything he says and does, indeed “the end for which God
created the world,” is to magnify his own glory for the joy of his creatures.
Everything else—election, judgment, redemption—is penultimate; the glory
of God is ultimate.20
The significance of this profound truth for preaching is twofold. As
stated at the beginning of this chapter, preachers should preach to glorify
God because everything we do, like everything God does, is supposed to
glorify God. Further, as Calvin understood, God’s passion for his glory is
the most comprehensive unifying theme in Scripture, a broader theological
perspective than, say, justification. Luther, looking for the latter, saw Christ
everywhere in the Bible. Calvin saw God everywhere.21
Theology proper is the preacher’s best lens for seeing and displaying the
unity of the Bible. Other lenses, like covenant, law-gospel, or redemptive-
historic, elucidate some texts but not all, or at least not all texts equally
well. We may appreciate, for example, the kingdom lens, but find that it
works better in the Synoptic Gospels than in large swaths of Scripture
where the kingdom theme is not prominent. Or we may appreciate a
traditional Lutheran lens, but discover that law and gospel are not present in
every text. Lutheran pastor Glenn Monson thinks they both need to be
present in every sermon. But why? If “it is rare that a text is fully law and
gospel, self-contained,”22 why insist that all texts be made to fit this
theological construct? God as central, on the other hand, is
comprehensively adequate because he is prominent everywhere. We can be
faithfully theocentric in every pericope because the Bible is, as Walter
Wangerin puts it, “The Book of God.”23
God of the New Testament?
Is God the God of the New Testament? Certainly. But the Old Testament
tells us much about God that is assumed and not repeated in the New.24 And
the gospel we hear in the New Testament “is not a message which broke de
novo upon the world with the appearing of Christ and the ministry of the

--- Page 91 ---
apostles.”25 It is the gospel that God promised beforehand in the Scriptures
(Rom. 1:2; Titus 2:1) and that was preached prior to the coming of the
Messiah (Heb. 4:2). It begins in Genesis, not Matthew. Congregants who
hear only from the New Testament are like moviegoers coming late into a
film, having missed essential information. “Unless we preach from the Old
Testament, our people really cannot know who their Lord is.”26
God as Redeemer?
Is God a Redeemer? Certainly. But a hermeneutical-homiletical theory
that privileges redemption may miss creation and wisdom themes in
Scripture. David Helm advocates theological reflection as an essential part
of sermon preparation but seems to limit this theologizing to showing how a
text relates to God’s plan of redemption.27 Why redemption only? A case
can be made that redemption is penultimate and that creation is at the heart
of the Bible’s theology: redemption leads to a restored paradise and
fellowship with our Creator.28
God the Son?
Is the Son of God himself God? Certainly. But texts “have other things to
say than ideas that fit easily with Christology.”29 Fred Craddock observes,
“Some in the Christian community seem content to supplant theology with
Christology, but perhaps unaware of the immense price: the dislodging of
Christ from salvation history, the loss of continuity with Israel’s faith, the
separation of creation from redemption (opening the door to every other-
worldly heresy hovering around the church), and the reduction of the first
item of the Christian creed to the role of preface.”30 Think about that last
phrase. “I believe in God the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth”
as mere preface? The long, wonderful story of God’s dealings with Adam,
Abraham, Sarah, Moses, David, and the prophets, and all the rich theology
in that narrative as preface? Unthinkable. But to listen to all the Jesus talk
and Jesus prayers and Jesus songs in some churches, you might think that
Jesus’s Father, whom he came to exalt, could be taken for granted. Or that
the Jesus story does not need to be set in a longer story of God’s dealings
with Israel. Or that faith in Jesus has replaced faith in God. Or that when we
confess Jesus as God, everyone knows what we mean by “God.” Or that if

--- Page 92 ---
our Christology is lofty enough and fervent enough, theology is
dispensable.
Preach the Gospel?
Do we need to preach the gospel? Certainly. But pulpit work is not
limited to the crucifixion and resurrection as concisely defined by Paul
(1 Cor. 15:1–5). We preach about money and marriage, parenting and
politics, gossip and gluttony, sex and sloth, and a host of other subjects. All
these subjects are, of course, preached after Good Friday and Easter; we do
not—or should not—preach “be good, do good” sermons, oblivious to
God’s initiative that precedes all our being and doing. But the themes of
preaching are larger than “Christ died, Christ arose.” The same apostle who
wrote, “We preach Christ crucified” (1 Cor. 1:23), could also say, “I have
not hesitated to proclaim to you the whole will of God” (Acts 20:27).
What did this “whole will of God” include? What did Paul cover during
his two years in Ephesus? “Anything that would be helpful to you,” he said
(Acts 20:20). Presumably, Paul’s ministry with this congregation included
creation themes as well as redemption themes, Wisdom literature as well as
messianic prophecy, didachē and paraklēsis as well as kerygma.
But even if we understand all Christian preaching as gospel preaching, if
“gospel” is an apt label for the content of every truly Christian sermon,
whatever the text or topic, the gospel we preach is the gospel of God (Mark
1:14; Rom. 1:1; 15:16; 2 Cor. 11:7; 1 Thess. 2:8–9; 1 Pet. 4:17). God is its
source and subject. God is our Savior (1 Tim. 1:1; 2:3; 4:10; 2 Tim. 1:8–9;
Titus 1:3; 2:10; 3:4; Jude 25). It is God’s grace that brings salvation (Titus
2:11). All things, Paul writes, come to us through Christ but from God; we
live through Christ but for God (1 Cor. 8:6).
This vital truth may be lost when preaching inadvertently sets Father and
Son over against one another in the gospel story. Though no pastor or
christocentric homiletician would ever knowingly encourage such a
perverted notion, some people imagine a drama of salvation in which Jesus
and God are on opposite sides. The Father—harsh, demanding, and
wrathful—is intent on judging us. But Jesus—kind, compassionate, and
merciful—comes to the rescue and offers himself as a sacrifice in our stead.
Who could blame anyone who thinks that this is the gospel for loving Jesus
but shrinking from God?

--- Page 93 ---
The gospel in Romans is the power of God for salvation (1:16). In it a
righteousness from God is made known, to which the law and prophets
testify (3:21). God presents Christ as an atoning sacrifice (3:25), God
credits righteousness (4:24), God demonstrates his love (5:8), God
foreknows, predestines, calls, justifies, and glorifies (8:29–30). People who
set Father and Son against each other in the drama of salvation do not
understand Paul’s gospel, wherein God, no less than Christ, is for us, not
sparing his Son but delivering him up for us all (8:31–32). “Oh, the depth of
the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! . . . From him and through
him and for him are all things. To him be the glory forever!” (11:33, 36).
So this letter to the Romans, where the church has found its clearest
exposition of the gospel, is manifestly about God. “God is the most
important word in this epistle.”31 In view of the God-centered content of
Romans, it seems pointless to ask whether the genitive in 1:1 means
“about” God or “from” God. It’s both.32
What we see in Romans we find elsewhere in the epistles. “God was
reconciling the world to himself in Christ” (2 Cor. 5:19). God made the
sinless Christ “to be sin for us” that we might be made the righteousness of
God in him (2 Cor. 5:21). Christian conversion is turning to God (Gal. 4:8–
9; 1 Thess. 1:8–9). God elects and adopts believers (Eph. 1); God makes the
gospel take root and grow in the human heart (1 Cor. 3:6); God regenerates
(Col. 2:13); God works in us to will and to do his good pleasure (Phil.
2:13). The gospel is good news about God: “When the kindness and love of
God our Savior appeared, he saved us . . . because of his mercy” (Titus 3:4–
5). The community of love and grace that results from the gospel is never
called in the New Testament the church of Jesus Christ, but the church of
God (1 Cor. 1:2; 10:32; 11:22; 15:9; 2 Cor. 1:1; Gal. 1:13; 1 Thess. 2:14;
1 Tim. 3:5). James Dunn is surely right: “The Christian gospel has to do
first and last and foremost with God.”33
For centuries, preachers followed Augustine’s allegorizing lead,
convinced, as he was, that every passage must be about Christ.34 But then
the Reformation came, rediscovering the plain sense of Scripture (we might
say it came because of the rediscovery). John Calvin was satisfied to preach
God from the Bible and thought that some who preached Christ from every
text, while meaning well, could do so only by doing violence to the plain
language of the Bible.35 Calvin did not think that even Job’s confession, “I
know that my Redeemer lives,” required reference to Christ; it was the

--- Page 94 ---
living God with whom Job had to do, and it was this God who would
redeem him and, in some way Job could only dimly guess, raise him. Some
Lutherans criticized Calvin’s insistence on the plain meaning of the text.
One even called him “Calvin the Judaizer” because when he preached from
the Old Testament he sometimes seemed to be saying no more than a
devout Jew would say on the same text.36
Calvin’s critics anticipated Jay Adams: “If you preach a sermon that
would be acceptable to the members of a Jewish synagogue . . . there is
something radically wrong with it.”37 Really? Read Calvin’s sermons on the
Psalms and see if there’s something radically wrong with them because he
feels no obligation to center on Christ if the psalm under consideration is
not plainly christological. Read, for that matter, the epistle of James: apart
from two passing references to Jesus, there’s nothing in this letter that a
devout Jew could not assent to. Perhaps Adams should say, “If you preach
only sermons that could be preached in the synagogue, never getting around
to the cross, and if your sermons are not set in worship services where
Christ is praised in song and prayers are offered in his name, then there is
something radically wrong with your preaching.”
A woman who visited our church several years ago said on her way out,
“It was nice to hear a sermon about God.” I thought at first she meant that
she was fed up with all the man-centered preaching out there nowadays, but
she explained, “Everywhere I go I hear preachers talking about Jesus, but
not too many talk about God.” That should not be. Christian theology is not
“consumed without remainder in Christology.”38
Homiletical Rationale
If on biblical-theological grounds preaching should be God centered, what
are the implications for the shape, content, and delivery of sermons? Here
are a few.
Sermons Should Exposit the God-Breathed Text
Perhaps this should go without saying, but preachers have no warrant for
speaking except that God has spoken, and they have nothing to say except
what God has said. Authoritative, God-glorifying preaching depends on
fidelity to the text: not just getting the drift of the passage and then talking

--- Page 95 ---
about it for twenty-five minutes, but grounding the message in the words of
Scripture, showing listeners where the assertions of the sermon come from
in the passage being exposited.39
Anyone who’s tried this knows how difficult it is! It’s easier to allegorize
Scripture (especially obscure texts) or to use it as a launch pad for what you
really want to say than to figure out its meaning and significance and
skillfully communicate that. But this is the hard work to which God has
called his heralds. We have to “devote [ourselves],” like Ezra, to studying,
observing, and teaching God’s Word (Ezra 7:10; 2 Tim. 2:15). Fortunately,
God does not leave us on our own. He gives us “a well-instructed tongue”
(Isa. 50:4) and grants insight if we’re willing to do the hard work of
thinking about what we read in Scripture (2 Tim. 2:7).
We glorify God when we faithfully explain, commend, and apply his
words with respect for the form, content, purpose, and tone of the passage
at hand. Anything and everything the preacher can do to grow in
faithfulness to the text of Scripture honors the God-centeredness of
Scripture.
Sermons Should Be Prepared and Delivered in the Power of
the Holy Spirit
By his Spirit God still speaks through what he has spoken. His Word is
the sword of the Spirit (Eph. 6:17) that cuts to the hearts of hearers (Heb.
4:12). Preachers dare not depend on intellect or eloquence (1 Cor. 2:4–16),
but must depend on the equipping, guidance, and enablement of the Spirit
(Acts 1:8; 4:31; 6:10). Preachers do not report on bygone days from an
antiquated book, but, authorized and emboldened by the Spirit, herald a
fresh, living word to their contemporaries (1 Pet. 1:12). When the power of
preaching is the power of the Spirit, God gets the glory (1 Cor. 2:4–5;
2 Cor. 4:7).
Implications of this conviction could fill books. One is that preachers
should never enter the pulpit without praying—praying all week while
meditating on the text (Ps. 119:125; 2 Tim. 2:7), praying for the filling of
the Holy Spirit while speaking (Acts 4:31), praying that the Spirit who
inspired those who wrote will now illumine those who hear (Ps. 119:18).
The story has been often told but may be worth retelling anyway:
supposedly, as Charles Spurgeon mounted the fifteen steps to the pulpit at

--- Page 96 ---
the Metropolitan Tabernacle, he muttered to himself on each step, “I believe
in the Holy Ghost.”40
God is central in preaching when people hear the living voice of his
Spirit in the proclamation of his inscripturated words.
Sermons Should Model Good Hermeneutics
Lay people learn hermeneutics from their pastors’ preaching. Whether
we like it or not, they learn how to interpret Scripture from how we handle
Scripture in the pulpit. So what do we teach listeners about hermeneutics
when Jesus makes a surprise appearance in a sermon from Proverbs? When
it turns out Song of Solomon is not really about God’s gift of married sex
but about Christ’s love for his church? When redemption trumps creation as
the theological underpinning of every sermon? When texts are not handled
with integrity because every Sunday the preacher follows the counsel to
“make a beeline to the cross”? Christocentric preaching may train people to
look past what’s plainly there in the text and to look instead for a reference
to Christ that may or may not be there. Christopher Wright says it bluntly:
“If you approach a text in the Old Testament with the assumption that ‘This
must be about Jesus,’ you easily overlook all that the original author was
actually trying to say. In fact, you end up putting a gag on the mouth of the
original author, in order to make him say what you think his text must be
about since it must be ‘all about Jesus.’ And that is a very bad thing to do to
the Bible!”41 Wright gives an example of a preacher who jumped from
Amos’s “Let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing
stream” (5:24) to “our only righteousness is in Christ,” which is true, but
not what Amos was saying. God’s voice through Amos was silenced in the
sermon because the preacher wanted to—thought he had to—talk about
something else. Another published sermon, this one on Psalm 30, takes the
superscription “For the dedication of the temple” to refer to Christ
dedicating the temple of his body on the cross, so that the whole sermon is
about Jesus’s joy in his resurrection. “That is a very bad thing to do to the
Bible.”
Careful advocates of christocentric hermeneutics avoid such egregious
misinterpretations. But I’ve heard or read enough sermons as misguided as
the ones cited above to fill the rest of this volume with examples. Time and

--- Page 97 ---
time again the plain meaning and thrust of a text is nudged aside to make
room for a christocentric reading.
I agree with Abraham Kuruvilla in his review of Sidney Greidanus’s
Preaching Christ from Genesis that Christology is not a sufficient base from
which to work on every text. “May not one discern a level of theology that
is more specific for, and closer to, the textual details? And could not one
make the move to application from that theological locus, rather than
aiming for a broad and general christocentric theological approach that does
not appear to be driven by the specifics of the text?”42
People have a right to expect that a sermon will say what the Bible says.
But if we import Christology (or law-gospel, or kingdom, or any other
theme) into texts, do we not unintentionally communicate that texts are
pretexts for talking about something else? Even if this something else is
Christ—the noblest subject conceivable—have we not compromised our
commitment to listeners that we will say what the text says? Theocentric
preaching avoids this problem because God never has to be read into any
Scripture text: every passage is from, for, and about God.
Sermons Should Be Weighty
If sermons come from the Word of God to confront listeners with the
reality of God for the glory of God, there ought to be in our preaching a
kind of glad gravity.43 Not unrelieved solemnity or humorlessness, but
intensity, a sense of blood-earnestness. Preacher and hearers are doing
business with the living God, the God who esteems the one who trembles at
his Word (Isa. 66:2). It’s hard to see how the kind of preaching J. I. Packer
calls “the chipper and chatty” can disclose God and nourish souls.44
On vacation one year, my family and I visited a “worship” service
featuring café tables where congregants drank coffee and ate Danish
pastries while a band played music so loud it would take the tartar off your
teeth. Then the preacher (in Bermuda shorts and untucked Hawaiian shirt)
delivered a talk, during which he moved in and out among the tables,
stopping for unrelated banter with members of the congregation, coming
back to the Bible on his stool to make what were, I’ll admit, orthodox,
practical observations from the text. On the way home, I asked my family
what they thought. Of course we talked about the music first (since worship
and music are virtually synonymous in American evangelicalism). Then I

--- Page 98 ---
asked, “What did you think of the conversational style of the sermon?” No
one spoke at first; then fifteen-year-old Jeff answered. Jeff, my ponytailed
actor-drummer with bare feet and ripped jeans, no clone of his buttoned-
down father, said, “It seems to me that a sermon ought to be big, because
God is big.”
For twelve years now, I’ve had that sentence hanging on the wall of my
study: “A sermon should be big because God is big.”
Applicational Rationale
A few suggestions and a few examples illustrate how a theocentric
approach informs the preacher’s efforts to faithfully apply the Bible to
listeners’ lives.
Use Theocentric Vocabulary When Interrogating the Text
Before a word of the sermon is written or spoken, the preacher can keep
the focus on God by asking applicational questions like the following:
“How are God’s character and will portrayed in this text? Why does God
intend to act this way? What does God call his people to be and do in this
text? Why does God call us to respond in this way? How does God want to
use this passage to shape the life of this congregation? How is God at work
in our situation, our moment in history?”45
Make God the Subject of Many of the Sermon’s Sentences
Instead of “We need to witness more,” say, “God wills and empowers our
witness.” Instead of “Marriage works best when husband and wife try to
outdo each other in sacrificial service,” say, “The God who sacrificed his
Son for us models how spouses are to love . . .”
The main points in the outline can help keep God central, as in a sermon
on Haggai 1:
1. God confronts his people about their misguided priorities (1:2–6)
2. God explains the need for his people to have right priorities (1:7–11)
3. God’s people change their priorities (1:12–15)46

--- Page 99 ---
Or this one on 1 Samuel 16:1–13 (Samuel’s anointing of David):
1. God provides for his kingdom (16:1)
2. God stoops to our fears (16:2–5a)
3. God prevents our folly (16:5b–7)
4. God reverses our conventions (16:8–13)47
Making God central in the skeleton of the exposition helps the preacher
avoid man-centered applications: do more, try harder. Application becomes
more clearly a matter of receiving and cooperating with what God is doing.
If not in every main point of the outline, we can at least, as Paul Scott
Wilson suggests, make God the subject of the proposition or big idea (what
he calls the sermon-in-a-sentence) and make an activity of God the main
verb of that proposition.48 “God sheds light,” “God knows what you’re
going through.” And so on.
Does this mean that God the Father is the only one who can act in these
sermon sentences? No. Elsewhere Wilson urges preachers to ask of every
passage, “What is God (in one of the persons of the Trinity) doing in this
biblical text?”49 So some sermons will be Christ-centered: “Christ is the end
of the law,” “Jesus never fails,” “Christ commands us to make disciples.” If
Christ is the main actor in the text, then he’ll appear as the subject of the
sermon’s focus sentence and that sermon will be christocentric. Other
sermons will highlight the role of the third person of the Trinity: “The Spirit
makes diverse groups one,” “The Spirit empowers every believer for
service,” and so on. Some sermons will not specify one person of the
Trinity. Wilson’s prescription for theological exegesis allows for many
christocentric sermons in the context of a theocentric pulpit ministry.
Naming God in at least some of the sermon’s key sentences should help
preacher and listener remember that whatever response the text calls for is a
response to God’s doing and in tandem with God.
Don’t Hesitate to Exhort
Christocentric scholars like Sidney Greidanus and practitioners like
Timothy Keller are certainly right to insist that the Bible is not a to-do list
or self-improvement manual. The primary burden of the gospel is not “try
harder,” or “be more like David or Moses,” or even “be more like Jesus.”

--- Page 100 ---
But Jason Hood and John Carrick, among others, raise a common critique
of christocentric, redemptive-historical preaching: this approach so
privileges the indicative that the imperative of Scripture is sometimes
suppressed.50 God the Spirit, who inspired the Scriptures, clearly wants
listeners to change in a variety of ways and speaks through the Bible’s
human authors “to teach us” (Rom. 15:4), that “the servant of God may be
thoroughly equipped for every good work” (2 Tim. 3:16–17), providing
biblical examples to follow or avoid (1 Cor. 10:6, 11; Heb. 4:11); in other
words, for moral formation of the people of God.
Some preachers are so afraid of “moralizing” or “exemplar preaching”
that they hesitate to ever speak in the imperative mode or suggest that we
can learn something from a Bible character’s virtues or flaws. Balance is
called for: by all means emphasize God’s gracious initiative in his dealings
with his human creatures. But his grace does not preclude exhortation, as
indicated by hundreds of scriptural commands, by our Lord’s proclamation,
“Repent and believe the good news” (Mark 1:15), and by the indicative-
imperative structure of some of Paul’s epistles. Ligon Duncan advocates
preaching Christ and Christian living from the Old Testament, unlike some
in the redemptive-historical school of preaching who think we should never
mine the Old Testament for examples to heed or avoid.51
Charles Simeon’s faithful expository preaching set a high standard for the
English-speaking pulpit in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth
centuries. And his patient training of young preachers left a lasting mark on
the Church in England and beyond. Simeon taught pastors to exhort as well
as instruct. The preface to his Horae Homileticae reads:
There is another point also, in respect to which it has been [Simeon’s] aim not to offend; and
that is, in not so perverting the Scripture as to make it refer to Christ and his salvation, when no
such object appears to have been in the contemplation of the inspired writer. He regrets to
observe, in some individuals . . . an ultra-Evangelical taste; which overlooks in many passages
the practical lessons they were intended to convey, and detects in them only the leading
doctrines of the Gospel. This error he has laboured earnestly to avoid; being well assured, that
the lessons of morality are, in their place, as useful and important as the doctrines of grace. In a
word, it has been his endeavour faithfully to deliver, in every instance, what he verily believed
to be the mind of God in the passage immediately under consideration. . . . He wishes much that
the practice of expounding the Scriptures, which obtained so generally, and with such beneficial
effects, at the time of the Reformation, were revived.52
I should note that not every text calls for action on the part of the hearer.
My colleague at Trinity Evangelical Divinity School, Greg Scharf, prefers

--- Page 101 ---
to speak of “response” to the text rather than “application” because the
latter term may imply that there is always a tangible action point for us to
“do” after we hear a sermon. Maybe God simply wants us to accept or
believe or rejoice or hope or imagine something.
Privilege the Text
The passage at hand will tell us how God wants us to apply it to our own
lives and the lives of our listeners. We do not need to privilege constructs
like law and gospel or redemptive history or ask how Christ might
somehow be found in the pericope in order to discern how to respond to its
message. In fact, doing so may result in every sermon sounding pretty much
the same, as in a recent devotional commentary on the Psalms: the author
misses the opportunity to apply Scripture in a rich variety of ways because
every exposition makes a turn to the cross and an appeal to trust Christ as
our only righteousness. I’ll grant that the propitiatory death and imputed
righteousness of Christ are hugely important truths, but they are not the
only truths the Psalms have to teach.53
Influential pastor C. J. Mahaney counsels preachers to focus on Calvary,
not just over the long course of ministry but in every single sermon
“regardless of the text or topic at hand.” Listeners “should anticipate the
sight of Calvary in every sermon and rejoice when it comes into view, and
all the more when the cross is not immediately obvious in the text.”54
Really? R. W. L. Moberly is more careful in advocating moves from text to
Christ: in Jesus, he notes, the Bible’s grand narrative finds its climax but not
its sole meaning.55
Earlier I cited Fred Craddock’s concern that, among other things that
result when Christ replaces God at the center of preaching, creation is
separated from redemption, “opening the door to every other-worldly
heresy hovering around the church.”56 What Craddock means can be
illustrated by an article in Creation Care: David P. Gushee laments the
failure of many Christians to think theologically about the environment
because their working theology is “privatistic, other-worldly, and
soteriological.” The gospel they embrace has “little or nothing to do with
this world.” Gushee links this narrow theological vision to the kind of
advice he and many other preachers got in seminary, to “preach the gospel
in every message through whatever text or issue happens to be before us,” a

--- Page 102 ---
gospel “in which the drama of personal salvation is all that really
matters.”57
What other subjects are preachers avoiding because an obvious
christological or soteriological connection can’t be discerned? Political
economy? The sanctity of life? Biomedical ethics? A God-exalting view of
vocation (beyond being a witness for Christ in the workplace)? Church life
may be pietistic and privatistic indeed unless preachers form a Christian
worldview and inform Christian action in the culture with a comprehensive
theological vision, one I believe is better cast by theocentrism than by
christocentrism. “To say that Christian preaching is or should be a
proclamation about God is to say something central to who we are, what we
are about, and how we regard the world in which we live out our faith, the
world God created and loves.”58
Here are a few sermon summaries that suggest directions theocentric
sermon application might take.
1 SAMUEL 17: THERE IS A GOD IN ISRAEL
Without retracting what I said earlier about finding legitimate moral
examples in the Bible, I would argue that the emphasis in the David and
Goliath story is not on us but on God. God is seen to be at work in this text,
replacing faithless Saul with a man after his own heart who will show the
watching world that there is a God in Israel. Should a sermon on 1 Samuel
17 emphasize, more specifically, the second person of the Godhead?
Edmund Clowney thinks so: he sketches an exposition that includes a move
toward Christ that doesn’t seem forced or artificial, but doesn’t add all that
much to the sermon, either. Clowney’s précis keeps the focus on God
instead of on David’s bravery or our ability to slay giants: a rich,
theologically faithful sermon-in-the-making, and it seems to me that
nothing essential is lost if we do not take the further step Clowney takes of
making David prophetic of his Greater Son. I disagree that “it is impossible
not to see Christ in this passage.”59
PSALM 72: A PRAYER FOR GOOD GOVERNMENT
Christopher Wright argues that though many Old Testament passages
have links to Christ, we should not turn a link into the main message. The
preacher can show how the message connects to Christ (and even this is not

--- Page 103 ---
always necessary), but the main message of the sermon should be the main
message of the text at hand.60 For example, Psalm 72 asks God to endow
Israel’s king with the virtues the king needs in order for the nation to
prosper. A sermon on this psalm will, of course, distinguish ancient Israel
from our own nation and dispensation, but should still major on our longing
for just, God-honoring government. Applications might include how we
vote, how we pray for leaders and hold them accountable, and how as
citizens of a republic we embody the civic virtues called for in the psalm. It
may be appropriate to end such a sermon by noting that only Jesus perfectly
fulfills the profile of the ideal ruler, perhaps leading into songs that worship
the King and anticipate his return. But to make the entire sermon about
Jesus, only Jesus (as one sermon I’ve heard and another I’ve read), is to
make the link the main message.61
MATTHEW 24:36–44: THE DAY AND HOUR UNKNOWN
Glenn Monson offers a sermon on this text as an example of his law and
gospel methodology.62 His theme is “To be prepared for Christ’s coming we
put on Christ,” and his aim is “to exhort believers to lay aside the garments
of sin and put on Christ daily.” But Matthew 24 says absolutely nothing
about garments or putting on Christ. Monson’s message and application
must be imported from other texts and are driven by his commitment to
finding law and gospel in every pericope. Indeed, he admits, “The gospel
move needed to be done with texts outside the appointed lessons.”63 A
theocentric approach (like Kuruvilla’s pericopal theology) will make
applications from the teaching of this passage and save exhortations to put
on Christ for a sermon on, say, Ephesians 4.
REVELATION 18:4: “COME OUT OF HER, MY PEOPLE”
I preached this text in 1999 with the main idea “Christians should avoid
the materialistic sins of Babylon.” While writing this chapter I was
preparing to preach the passage again and thought I ought to take my own
advice. So I reworded the main idea: “God calls his people to flee the
materialistic sins of Babylon.” This subtle but important change helped me
keep a theocentric focus in application: spiritual adultery, excessive luxury,
and the hubris of Babylon are an affront to God. This theocentric focus is

--- Page 104 ---
only right; it is God, after all, who speaks the words “Come out of her, my
people.”
Conclusion
James Stewart (adapting William Temple) wrote that the aim of all true
preaching is “to quicken the conscience by the holiness of God, to feed the
mind with the truth of God, to purge the imagination by the beauty of God,
to open the heart to the love of God, to devote the will to the purpose of
God.”64 I can’t say it any better than that. Let God be central in the
preaching of his Word.

Response to Kenneth Langley
BRYAN CHAPELL
The sound principles of biblical interpretation in Ken Langley’s work make
it rich reading. For example, he begins with a statement that I would hope
all believers would endorse: “Preaching should be God centered because
God is God centered and wants us to be God centered in everything we do”
(81). That healthy summary of 1 Corinthians 10:31 should elicit a loud
“Amen.”
The question Langley raises is whether “Christ-centered preaching”
diverges from this “God-centered” ethic. As Langley helpfully offers, “It’s
one thing to say the prophets point to Christ, another to say every word they
wrote is about Christ” (83). Again, we should offer a hearty “Amen.” No
credible advocate of biblical theology argues that “every word” of the
prophets is about Christ. The caricature may seem to be supported by
writers who advocate finding “Jesus on every page,” but a close reading
will indicate their emphasis is showing how the messages of the prophets
(or the law, or Wisdom literature, etc.) consistently “point to Christ,” not
how every text mentions Jesus.
Of course, what it means to “point to” Christ also needs explanation.
Helpful to me in this task is Langley’s reminder that whether one is talking
about the prophets or the law, what it means “to be faithful to this part of
Scripture [is] to focus on what it tells us about our Creator-Redeemer” (84;
emphasis added). Summarizing his biblical argument, Langley writes, “God
is at the heart of redemptive history. He is its main character” (88–89). This
is exactly right, and my hope is that Langley does not mean that God the
Father alone is the main character. That would seem to undermine the
trinitarian egalitarianism that Langley wants to protect. Rather, the
trinitarian God uses the Scriptures to “point to” himself as the Creator who
redeems (e.g., Gen. 1:1; John 1:1–5; Col. 1:16–20; Heb. 1:1–3).
The right apprehension of God who is “Creator-Redeemer,” rather than
the exclusion of any member of the Godhead, is the commendable goal of
Langley’s theological rationale. Such a rationale rightly dispenses with

--- Page 109 ---
scriptural interpretations that are about “redemption only,” or “have [no]
other things to say than ideas that fit easily with Christology” (91),1 or are
“limited to the crucifixion and resurrection” (91), or “look past what’s
plainly there in the text and . . . look instead for a reference to Christ that
may or may not be there” (97). All of these caricatures, Langley graciously
acknowledges, “careful advocates of christocentric hermeneutics avoid”
(97). So, because we agree on what needs to be avoided, it is important to
identify the theological rationale of those who practice christocentric
preaching as “careful advocates.”
The previously presented theological rationale (see “Redemptive-Historic
View,” above) of the twentieth century’s greatest biblical theology
advocate, Geerhardus Vos, is in harmony with Langley’s interpretive aim.
Vos says that the primary object of God’s revelation is himself. By his
recorded dealings with his creation and creatures, God is revealing himself
so that he will be properly understood, honored, and obeyed.
In order for this revelation to unfold as God intended through the course
of time required the canon of Scripture; the process is progressive. Vos says
that our knowledge of God becomes more clear and thorough as Scripture’s
truths advance toward the dawn of God’s fullest disclosure of himself in
exact representation (Col. 1:15; cf. John 14:7, 9; Heb. 1:3).
The organic nature of the progressive process must also be understood to
interpret God’s revelation of himself. The earlier aspects of revelation are
laying foundations and filters for our understanding of the later aspects, and
the later revelation provides explanation and rationale for the earlier
aspects.
These progressive and organic features do not exhaust the nature of
God’s revelation because they do not explain the nonexhaustive nature of
Scripture. The Bible does not explain everything that could be said about
God’s dealings with his creation and creatures. As the apostle John writes of
the second member of the Trinity, “Now there are also many other things
that Jesus did. Were every one of them to be written, I suppose that the
world itself could not contain the books that would be written” (John 21:25
ESV).
So if the Bible does not reveal all that God is and does, what is the
selection criterion for the material the divine Author inspires? The answer
offered from Scripture is that God’s revelation is distinctly redemptive. The
protevangelium (i.e., the “first gospel” of Gen. 3:15) declares the distinct

--- Page 110 ---
nature of the light of revelation that must grow toward the dawning of
Christ’s person and work (Luke 24:27; Rom. 16:20; Gal. 3:8). God reveals
himself in the context of the disclosures of his grace that flow through the
entirety of Scripture and culminate in Christ (Rom. 15:4; cf. Matt. 12:21;
Col. 1:27).
This redemptive flow does not force us to claim that Jesus is mentioned
in every verse of the Bible, but it helps us discern his necessity and nature
based upon the aspects of grace that precede him, describe him, and explain
his claim upon our hearts and lives. Assertions that this redemptive
perspective disregards individual texts’ details or that it elbows out mention
of other members of the Trinity are due more to misapplications of biblical
theology than to any systemic problem with the approach.
Biblical theology simply insists that we interpret texts in their intended
context. Christ’s revelation does not displace the Father, but discloses him
(John 14:9; 2 Cor. 4:6). The Holy Spirit is not devalued in Christ’s
revelation but fulfilled in it (John 14:26; 15:26; 16:13). Through the
scriptures that predict, prepare for, reflect, and result from the ministry of
Christ, we discern the fullness of God and our obligations to him (Col. 2:9;
1 John 1:1–4). As Langley rightly observes, God is not merely revealing
himself as Creator, but as Creator-Redeemer. To do less in our
interpretations is to proclaim less than God reveals.
Christ is essential not only to understanding our Creator-Redeemer’s
nature but also to obeying his commands (John 14:15). The right
application of all biblical texts will not allow us to ignore any of their
imperatives nor the inability in us that their perfections reveal (Gal. 2:19;
3:11–12, 24) nor the necessity of Christ’s Spirit in us that their performance
requires (John 15:5; Gal. 2:20; 1 John 4:4). The grace of God that is
progressively revealed in Scripture until it comes to full display in Christ
does not excuse us from obedience to him, but binds our hearts in love and
loyalty to his glory and enabling power (Titus 2:11–12).

PAUL SCOTT WILSON
We write from our contexts. Ken Langley’s well-argued point is that the
Bible is about God, and sermons should be theocentric, not christocentric.
Preachers are to be like Isaiah 40:9 and say to Zion, “Here is your God.”
The church Langley has served since 1999 is in Zion (Illinois, not Israel); it
is named after Christ, Christ Community Church; the huge sanctuary is
dominated by a cross in front of the chancel window; the sanctuary is
triangular for the Trinity; and of nine statements of belief, eight mention or
imply Christ and the ninth concerns the authority of the Word. There may
be little danger Christ is not central. My guess is that Langley has heard too
many “just Jesus” sermons that ignore God.
Langley is not against christocentric sermons (one could be forgiven for
misreading him on this). He is against (1) preaching that is always
christocentric and (2) sermons that use artificial ways to make every text
about Christ. His caution that both Testaments are theocentric, as is Jesus’s
preaching, is worthy, to a point. God is the main actor. Even in the
incarnation, resurrection, and second coming, it is God acting, but Christ is
central.
Do theocentric and christocentric need to be such stark alternatives?
Theologians like Daniel Migliore reclaim the early church fathers’ notion of
perichoresis—commingling, indwelling, or some say dancing—to describe
the relationship of the Trinity. It is a model of community love, three
individuals in one indivisible being. It is a union of mutual self-giving
without loss of personal distinction. When the Trinity is seen this way, to be
theocentric is to be christocentric, and Spirit-centric as well. I have always
been grateful to a mentor who was ridiculously practical as well as biblical
and said preachers should use trinitarian language in sermons because
people need as many ways to conceive of God as possible.
By way of personal testimony, I believe because the Jesus testified to by
Scripture, who died, was raised, ascended, intercedes for us in the Spirit,
and is testified to by the church—this same Christ has met and brought me

--- Page 114 ---
into newness of life. I do not say, I am created, therefore I am Christian.
Creation is a constant source of wonder and praise, but it does not with
certainty disclose God’s full nature. God’s victory over all the forces of
death is the starting place of my faith, so for faith renewal each week, I
need the preacher to connect the truth of a pericope to the larger saving
actions of God in the entire Christ event, and most centrally in the cross and
resurrection. This is not to reduce everything to the Christ event, but to
recognize that all is infused with it: “I am making all things new” (Rev.
21:5). For me, that promise locks in my faith. The preacher’s “Behold your
God” becomes my “Praise God.” The issue is not that preachers must
always get to Christ, but rather, why would they not make some link to
Christ if it can strengthen faith?
Would it be helpful to talk about different ways Christ can be featured in
sermons? “Christocentric” can mean different things, including negatively:
ignoring what a text says about God, or reducing every text to personal
redemption, or making every text to be about only Jesus. By contrast,
Christ-centered preaching can honor the unity of the redemptive message of
the Bible. It can honor the good news in each text and then make links to
the entire Christ event, with the cross and resurrection as central, either
typologically or with echoes of other biblical texts and doctrines. This can
be a way to further ground and amplify the gospel in any text.
“Law” and “gospel” in history have sometimes been read as a template a
preacher lays on a text. The baggage those terms carry is one reason I prefer
to use “trouble” and “grace” instead. The gospel comes to us as an event
and consists of both trouble and grace, united in a tensive relationship. It is
not, as modernity portrayed, a series of kerygmatic propositions left once
the rest of the New Testament is boiled off. The gospel is rather God who
comes to us, often in a signature movement found in many events: sin to
redemption, sickness to healing, hunger to feeding, oppression to liberation,
despair to hope, exodus to promised land, cross to resurrection, and fall to
new Jerusalem—basic movements of faith. They represent God’s saving
action. They are two sides of the same coin, two aspects of one indivisible
Word that simultaneously imply what humans are to do and what God does,
both by instruction and by gracious empowerment. To read Scripture with
trouble and grace in mind is to read the entire Bible as the gospel of God. It
is not to opt for a binary “either-or” (preaching one or the other), but a
tensive “both-and.” Variety should come from the range of texts we preach

--- Page 115 ---
and from various ways of arranging the sermon, not from whether or not the
gospel is preached. To my mind, to read without trouble and grace in mind
is not a neutral stance or objective way of reading Scripture, as the modern
world defined things; it is a way to disregard the gospel that is latent in each
text.
I love Langley’s recommendation: “The preacher can keep the focus on
God by asking applicational questions like the following: ‘How are God’s
character and will portrayed in this text? Why does God intend to act this
way? What does God call his people to be and do in this text? Why does
God call us to respond in this way? How does God want to use this passage
to shape the life of this congregation? How is God at work in our situation,
our moment in history?’” (99). Not all of these are applicational, however.
The first three of the six, dealing with the biblical text, are textual: they are
what the text is about, the revelation of who God is and who we are.
Langley rightly looks in texts for “what God has said,” presumably in both
word and act, for God speaks by acting, acts by speaking, and speaks to
explain acts.
The last three questions deal with us in the present, and thus are
application. Concerning the Glenn Monson sermon on Matthew 24:36–44:
the theme “To be prepared for Christ’s coming we put on Christ” not only
distorts the text, as Langley notes, but it also puts the burden on humans
(i.e., we must put on Christ). It is yet only trouble. Gospel as God’s saving
action is missing, which I would say is that “God (or Christ, or the Son of
Man) is coming at any moment.” From an applicational perspective, this is
good news for anyone who longs for the end of the bad deeds (as in Noah’s
time) and daily drudgery (like field labor and grinding grain). In Christ we
have One who has awakened us to his having come, to his presence with us
now, and to his coming again.
Concerning Langley’s own sermon on “Come out of her, my people”
(Rev. 18:4): his theme is, “God calls his people to flee the materialistic sins
of Babylon” (106). The message may be theocentric, but it still depends on
humans answering the call, without naming God’s help. To show the text to
be good news, one could say, “God saves God’s people.” The text refers to
removing them from Babylon before the city and its sinful ways are
destroyed. There is no reference to the cross, but applicationally, we have
certainty in faith of this saving action only because God in Christ conquered
death. Why not name and proclaim that?

Law-Gospel View
PAUL SCOTT WILSON
The hermeneutic I use in preaching is law and gospel, or, to use somewhat
less contentious words, trouble and grace. I assume that the Word of God is
dual edged: it both condemns and liberates, binds and frees. The same Word
does both. I also assume that the purpose of preaching is to proclaim the
gospel, which I define as God’s saving actions recorded anywhere in the
Bible that have greatest clarity in Jesus Christ. This purpose is larger than
preaching any individual biblical text. Bible texts are treasures in
themselves, yet they also provide windows or portals through which we
may view the larger faith story. These assumptions have important
implications for preaching, because they encourage sermons to be in fact
good news, to be vehicles of Christ through the Holy Spirit.
Biblical and Theological Rationale
From my childhood, many things helped shape my eventual homiletic,
including the weekly preaching of my father. He taught me not least about
Bible and theology, about the sermon’s link to pastoral care, about the value
of story, and, by negative example, about not leaving preparation to
Saturday night. However, two of the most distinct lessons came from my
grandmother and my mother, respectively.
Our family lived out on the prairies, and visits from relatives were few
and far between. Grannie Scott would come from Northern Ireland every
second summer. My feelings were mixed, because she would insist on
having daily devotions with the three of us grandchildren, and these often
fell in the middle of a game of baseball on the street or neighborhood hide-
and-seek. I nonetheless remain indebted to her, because in one of those

--- Page 117 ---
sessions she told me the story of the death of my grandfather, whom I never
met, and thereby she introduced me to grace.
She had been in the kitchen of their Methodist manse in Quebec
preparing lunch. Grandpa Thomas was up in his study on the second floor,
preparing his Sunday sermons. “Thomas,” she called, signaling that lunch
was ready. After a while she called again, with still no answer. The third
time she dried her hands on her apron and went up to get him. His study
door was closed, as was normal. As she placed her hand on the doorknob,
she heard a distinct voice: “My grace is sufficient for thee” (2 Cor. 12:9
KJV). She opened the door and found he had died, slumped over on his
rolltop desk. Because she heard what she understood to be God’s voice and
had that assurance of grace, she said, she was sustained not only through the
time of grieving but also for the rest of her life. Grace, I came to
understand, is not just an abstract idea, but God’s unconditional action of
unmerited favor.
My mother taught me a second lesson about preaching when she taught
me basic principles of baking and cooking, like don’t add flour to water, but
add water to flour so the dough won’t get lumpy. To avoid lumpy gravy,
don’t initially add hot water to the hot roux; first add ice water and mix.
When I arrived as a student in homiletics class, I needed to know the
basic sermon ingredients and principles for how they worked. What was I
trying to do, write an essay or a lecture? Make a speech? Be a pastor or a
prophet? What was the nature of God’s Word? What about grace? I wanted
to avoid lumpy sermons, and my questions led me to consider what the
Bible and theology tell us about law and gospel and about the bifocal nature
of the Word.
Law and Gospel
Law and gospel are essential ways to speak of what is at the heart of
faith. Law is a gift from God, yet it mainly emphasizes human action and
what we must do. Humans are required to change, yet we cannot do so on
our own. As Jesus says in John 15:5, “Apart from me you can do nothing.”
Paul says in 2 Corinthians 3:5, “Not that we are competent of ourselves to
claim anything as coming from us; our competence is from God.” Because
we cannot save ourselves or accomplish our mission and outreach on our
own, God by grace provides what is required in Jesus Christ through the

--- Page 118 ---
Spirit. Gospel is God’s actions of unmerited and unconditional grace on our
behalf. God’s saving action may be found in either Testament, but is most
clearly seen in Christ’s incarnation, ministry, crucifixion, resurrection, and
ascension; in the descent of the Spirit; in Christ’s second coming; and in his
eternal rule at the right hand of God. Good Friday and Easter are at the
heart of it all.
However, “law” and “gospel” are loaded terms. Like the long railway
trains that rumble across the prairies, they carry a lot of freight. “Law” and
“gospel” can mean many things. Some folks hear “Old Testament” and
“New Testament,” or “old covenant” and “new covenant.” Paul speaks
extensively of law and gospel in Romans and of “the ministry that brought
condemnation” and “the ministry that brings righteousness” (2 Cor. 3:6–9
NIV). Some hear various other words as synonyms for “law”: “God’s
commandments,” “teachings,” “instructions,” “sin” (our personal and
corporate transgressions of the law), “judgment” (the consequences of our
sin), “fallenness,” and “brokenness” (i.e., because of original sin the world
and society are not as God intended). Some may hear synonyms for
“gospel” in terms like “grace,” “salvation,” “justification,” “sanctification,”
“righteousness,” “the church’s message,” and “the Word.”
Law and gospel terminology may raise denominational flags. Some hear
law and gospel as particularly Lutheran, which they are, yet they are central
also for Calvin and Wesley. Luther’s uses of the law were (1) in its civil or
punitive function, to warn, condemn, restrain evil, curb behavior, and
provide a measure of justice, and (2) as a mirror of God’s perfection and a
judgment of human weakness in need of God’s help. Calvin reversed those
two and added a third use of the law: as a guide to stir in the righteous a
willingness or excitement to obedience. (Scholars say the third use is
present in Luther as well, though he did not call it that or emphasize it for
fear it would be misunderstood.) Calvin finds the third use in David in the
Psalms: “The prophet proclaims the great usefulness of the law: the Lord
instructs by their reading of it those whom he inwardly instills with a
readiness to obey. He [David] lays hold not only of the precepts, but the
accompanying promise of grace, which alone sweetens what is bitter. . . .
David especially shows that in the law he apprehended the Mediator,
without whom there is no delight or sweetness.”1 For Calvin, God is present
in the law. It is a gift that reveals human error, yet it also anticipates the

--- Page 119 ---
grace and contains a hint or promise of the sweetness of the gospel when
human action corresponds with God’s will.
Law and gospel are also Methodist. John Wesley attacked antinomians
who preached only law or only gospel. He says of the law that it slays some
—yet for others it is a gift, privilege, and wonderful liberty. Gospel is the
love of Christ. Preaching law and gospel is “the scriptural way, the
Methodist way, the true way.”2 Evangelical Episcopal theologian Paul F. M.
Zahl recently devoted a third of his Grace in Practice to law and gospel,
essentially understanding law as righteousness by works and gospel as
salvation by grace. He says of God’s grace, “No apologies! One-way love is
the heart of Christianity. It is what makes Christianity Christian.”3
Law and gospel represent a dynamic relationship, a necessary tension,
not mutually exclusive alternatives. The law is not a straw argument made
irrelevant by the subsequent gospel. As Jesus said, “I have not come to
abolish [the law and the prophets] but to fulfill” (Matt. 5:17). Law and
gospel are both true, they are both needed to represent the truth, yet they
may seem contradictory. As Luther said, simul justus et peccator, we are in
the same moment both saved and sinners. Neither half on its own is quite
adequate. To resolve the tension established between the two, faith is
needed. By holding law and gospel adjacent to one another, faith may find a
foothold. As Paul instructs, “Work out your own salvation with fear and
trembling; for it is God who is at work in you, enabling you both to will and
to work for his good pleasure” (Phil. 2:12–13).
“Law” and “gospel” are not binary terms, like “bad” and “good.” They
are not strict opposites. They are intertwined. Law is good, it is a gift, it is a
route to holiness. A warning is often a helpful gift, even if we still must
obey. As a command, it strikes humans initially as a burden, perhaps even
as impossible. When a drug addict is told to quit drugs, the instruction is
good, yet if that is all that is given, it is not enough to save. The addict may
be pointed in the right direction but is still hopelessly stuck. Law
accompanied by gospel, however, is empowerment through the Spirit to
make the required behavioral change. Both law and gospel are needed.
Swiss Reformed theologian Emil Brunner once marvelously said, God
meets us in the law, but “does not meet us as himself.”4 By the same token,
if we preach only gospel, God does not fully meet us in it. The good news is
irrelevant if it is not specific to human needs. Christ meets us fully as

--- Page 120 ---
himself in the pairing of law and gospel, in both command and
empowerment.
“Law” and “gospel” are not always easy terms. Sometimes I opt for
alternate terms like “trouble” and “grace,” although the law is not
appropriately reduced simply to trouble. Still, trouble and grace can provide
a simpler route to the preaching of the good news. They have less history
and carry less freight. “Trouble” needs little definition because most of us
know what it is and what it feels like. Trouble is difficulty. At the simplest
level, trouble (like law) puts the burden on humans to do something.
Alternatively, at the simplest level, grace (like gospel) puts the burden on
God, who has already accepted it in Jesus Christ. Preachers can use trouble
and grace to make an initial diagnosis of sermon material. To discover if
something is trouble or grace, ask: Does the burden to do something fall on
humans or on God? Does it feel like bad news or good news? Generally, if
something feels heavy, it is trouble. If it feels like good news, it may be
gospel. Law is a gift and reveals something of the character of God.
Trouble/law in practice is nonetheless basically anthropocentric, looking to
human action, while grace/gospel is theocentric, pointing to God’s saving
ways. In the sermon, each must be given time to be fully experienced by the
congregation.
To summarize, trouble or law on its own is not the gospel, though it
contributes to it. Grace on its own is also not the gospel, because grace is
God’s action saving us from something. We may thus say: gospel = trouble
(law) + grace.
The Need for a Focus on God
When I began teaching homiletics in seminary, a problem became
apparent early: many of the sermons were strong by some standards. The
students carefully studied the text, did good exegesis, identified key terms,
relied upon biblical commentators and theologians, were guided by
denominational standards, used good images and metaphors and stories,
ensured social and cultural relevance, and so forth. The product was often
sermons that left me feeling flat. After a few months, I began to recognize
the problem. The sermons were biblical but did not get to God. Or, if they
did get to God, they did not get to the gospel. God was portrayed primarily
as judge. Hearers most often were left carrying greater burdens after the

--- Page 121 ---
sermon than they had at the start. Quantities of “must, should, have to, and
ought” were hung out on the sermon clothesline. Some students sounded
more like police than pastors.
Something more was needed. With the text of David and Goliath (1 Sam.
17), one might preach all day on grammatical, literary, and historical
aspects of the text. The Philistines had a military advantage in possessing
the formula for iron. Their weapons were Iron Age, but Israel was trapped
in the Bronze Age, and one might say that David was still in the Stone Age.
Still, from a theological perspective, if God did not guide the pebble that
David launched from his slingshot, if God did not cause the Philistine army
to panic, if Israel did not believe that God saved them, if the story did not
reveal something about God’s nature, there would be no obvious reason to
preach that story, even though it is in the Bible. If any biblical text becomes
just history and bears no theology, if it has no application to faith and action
today, does it yet function as the Word of God?
If you purchase a Ford Focus, you may suddenly notice how many are on
the roads. If we note that preaching and the gospel are not identical, in
listening to sermons we may become aware of all the sermons in which the
gospel is missing. People are the focus. The emphasis is on what we
humans are to do, essentially using our own resources. The imperative
mood is common. It makes requests or commands, expecting the listener to
obey. Some preachers need encouragement to use the indicative mood,
which, by contrast, does not make demands and is thus easier for listeners
to receive. It typically describes, expresses an opinion, or inquires about
something.
What we humans do is important, and the Word always calls us to some
form of change or repentance. The weakness of sermons that center on
human actions is that God and grace are largely absent. This is the problem
with most published and internet sermons, irrespective of denomination.5
The burden falls on the listener alone to do what is needed. As an easy test
to verify this, review sermons and see how many of the sentences have
God, in one of the Three Persons, as the subject of the sentence. Do a
further check to see how many of those sentences actually stress God’s
helping action (as opposed to instruction or warning).
Even sermons that do get to grace often err in one of several directions:

--- Page 122 ---
1. God’s love is unconditional, but the preached grace is conditional, as
in “if you will change,” or “when we follow Christ we will discover,”
or “if you become good enough . . .” Good news that is dependent
upon us or that is only conditional as a promise in the future may
never arrive. Paul said, “For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I
do not want is what I do” (Rom. 7:19). If that was true for him, how
much more true is it for us? Far better it is to remind listeners that
they have already encountered God’s grace in baptism, prayer,
worship, community, service, and so forth. The good news needs to
be extended to the past and present if listeners are to trust it awaits in
the future.
2. Most sermons that mention grace do so only for a short period,
perhaps just at the end, not long enough for listeners even to begin to
visualize or comprehend it, or to begin tasting the sweetness Calvin
mentioned.
3. Even sermons based on biblical texts with an obvious gospel
emphasis are turned into law. Thus the parable of the lost sheep in
Luke 15:1–7 turns into a sermon on being diligent in searching out
those who are lost. That can be part of the message, but it is not yet
the gospel. Jesus focuses on God’s diligence in seeking the lost. God
will come looking even if only one is lost out of a hundred. This text
also points to how the preacher might link the sermon to the larger
gospel story: Jesus is determined to accomplish for us on the cross
what we cannot accomplish for ourselves (see, for instance, Jesus
setting his face to go to Jerusalem in Luke 9:31, 51).
In sum, a preacher can preach the Bible and not arrive at God’s Word, or
can preach God’s Word as law and not arrive at the gospel. Preaching often
puts the burden on humanity, as though Christ’s life, death, and resurrection
make no difference to the present day.
The Bifocal Nature of the Word
The Word of God both binds and frees. It is bifocal, law and gospel.
They are in a dynamic, tensive relationship. The dual-edged Word both
condemns and frees, cuts and restores. Luther, Calvin, and Wesley had no
set homiletic based on this, except that law was to convict the sinner, and

--- Page 123 ---
gospel was to encourage and sustain the repentant.6 John Wesley said law
and gospel can be preached “both at once” or “in their turns.”7 The great
Baptist preacher Charles Spurgeon in the late 1800s condemned any sermon
that does not offer the grace of God: “It sweeps over men’s heads like a
cloud, but it distributes no rain upon the thirsty earth.”8
The common assumption seems to be that human action and God’s action
will balance themselves out over a year. Yet they do not. Law dominates
and the gospel is occasional and often fleeting. If a preacher in the sermon
goes mainly once to the biblical text, as in sermons roughly patterned on the
Puritan plain-style method of exposition/application, nine times out of ten,
the received word will be corrective, something humans must do. In other
words, that form that involves a single movement to the text encourages a
theology of judgment or trouble. Frankly, most preachers do not receive
training to also look for the gospel. They may preach it from time to time,
not least at funerals, but may not have the right lenses to know it or to shape
it for effective Sunday preaching. For them, law and gospel have not yet
developed as hermeneutical tools.
Trouble and grace primarily are a grammar, not a form, for preaching.
They are the deep grammar of both theological discourse and sermons. In
language studies, grammar provides normative rules for clear expression. In
sermons, trouble and grace as grammar provide normative rules for clear
expression of the gospel. Like any grammar, these principles are not
obvious on the surface; they do not draw attention to themselves. Yet just as
sentences can be analyzed for arrangements of subject, predicate, object,
and so forth, a sermon can be analyzed according to how it employs law
and gospel and in what patterns. Is the burden on people or on God?
In addition to grammar, trouble-to-grace can be understood secondarily
as a possible structure for the sermon. Faith and the gospel have an
underlying plot, seen over and over in the Bible in the movement from
expulsion out of Eden in Genesis to entry into the new Jerusalem in
Revelation, from the exodus to the promised land, from crucifixion to
resurrection, from Good Friday to Easter, from the old creation to the new,
from the old covenant to the new, from sin to forgiveness, from
homelessness to homecoming, from affliction to health, and so forth. We
see this movement in Paul’s own experience in Asia: “We were so utterly,
unbearably crushed that we despaired of life itself. Indeed, we felt that we
had received the sentence of death so that we would rely not on ourselves

--- Page 124 ---
but on God who raises the dead. He who rescued us from so deadly a peril
will continue to rescue us” (2 Cor. 1:8–10). There are opposite theological
movements in the Bible, like backsliding from righteousness to
unrighteousness, or from justice to injustice, but those are the movements of
sin and evil, not of God. In other words, the gospel has a distinctive plot
that can be the underlying drive of the sermon.
Homiletical Rationale
The Sermon in Two Parts
I suggest that the sermon be conceived in two parts, moving from law
(trouble) to gospel (grace), not the other way around. A sermon might
present only grace, but God’s saving action independent of any trouble is
not truly saving. Preaching forgiveness without repentance is what Dietrich
Bonhoeffer called “cheap grace.” Grace becomes good news in relationship
to the law and when it takes account of troubles. Similarly, a sermon that is
just law, instructions, and demands will likely not inspire faith and reliance
upon God. When law and gospel are preached in sequence, however, by the
end of the sermon, trouble is not erased but it is transformed into an
invitation to rely upon God’s empowering resources for what is needed.
The movement from law to gospel is the essential plot of faith, and hence
of the sermon, giving the sermon interest and suspense. It creates tension
and resolution. It flows from naming the deep struggles of life to naming
the gospel in response to them.
The trouble-to-grace paradigm is not to be confused with
problem/solution. We would never speak of the movement from Good
Friday to Easter as problem/solution. The move is from trouble to a restored
relationship with God in Christ. What about preaching law and gospel back
and forth, over and over? Back and forth over and over establishes neither
and can be experienced like a train in a shunting yard: there is little forward
movement. Neither law nor gospel is experienced in depth. It is like
cleaning a wound, putting on a bandage, removing it, and repeating. Or it is
like probing something emotionally painful, saying some superficial words
to make it better, and then doing it again, probing and soothing without
going deep. It is better that people experience the depth of their sin or the
broken social systems in the world, and then move to the good news, taking

--- Page 125 ---
full account of the trouble. Much African American preaching does this,
ending in celebration of God’s action. It moves toward joy, and as
Ephesians instructs, it “equip[s] the saints for the work of ministry” (4:12).
One could argue that even if a sermon does not get to the gospel, the rest
of the service will, for instance in the prayers, singing, banners, videos,
sacraments, and so forth. Why worry about law and gospel? My response is
that if the gospel is barely represented in individual Sunday sermons, when
will the congregation have a chance to focus on what God is doing and
where they might expect to encounter God today?
At the grocery store, it is good to be guided by a shopping list and not by
cravings of the moment. A little discipline is needed. The same is true for
preaching. For the gospel to be preached weekly, the approach needs to be
intentional. Gospel requires a deliberate homiletic, not one guided merely
by whim—by whether, for instance, a preacher is discouraged or happy
when composing the sermon. Gospel requires discipline and an intentional
hermeneutic.
A Gospel Hermeneutic
Biblical preachers are taught to strive for objectivity when doing
exegesis, that process involving translation and issues of authorship,
historical setting, key terms, original meanings, and so forth. The exegetical
process tries to safeguard against imposing meanings on texts. The text
determines the sermon’s message. Still, something beyond what the text
says must also operate if one preacher finds only trouble in a text and
another finds both law and gospel. The difference may be explained by
what hermeneutic or method of interpretation is employed. The first
preacher uses a law hermeneutic, and the second uses a law-gospel
hermeneutic. In other words, what a preacher sees in a text is determined
not only by the text but also by the lenses used to read it, whether the
preacher is conscious of them or not. The question for us here becomes, Are
preachers warranted in using a gospel hermeneutic?
Since ancient times, the church has used the regula fidei, the rule of faith,
to guide interpretation of Scripture. For most of history, even including after
the Reformation, the church used “the four senses of Scripture” as
theological lenses to understand its texts. The literal sense of the text was
the grammatical meaning of the words and the historical events they

--- Page 126 ---
represented. The moral sense was claimed as the main meaning because it
gave even simple people practical guidelines on how to improve their souls
by behaving as God wills. Without using the term “moral sense,” the church
invokes it whenever it draws from a text a behavioral purpose, even if that
is not the text’s direct thrust. All preaching texts imply something for
human behavior. Our preaching ancestors also had two spiritual senses of
Scripture: the allegorical sense, which finds a meaning about Jesus Christ,
and the anagogical sense, which finds a meaning concerning the soul and
heaven and hell.
The four senses were precritical tools for reading Scripture theologically.
Today we rely on critical scholarship of the Bible and have more than four
senses or lenses in play. Lenses are provided by social and cultural contexts,
including race, gender, economic status, and so forth. Additional lenses are
provided by theologies: denominational, black, feminist, liberation,
narrative, Minjung, postcolonial, and others. Some of the ancient insights
nonetheless remain important. The Reformers claimed that the literal sense
of Scripture was the only true sense. They wanted to limit allegory. In
actual fact they maintained a “double-literal sense.” The grammatical-
historical or literal meaning was not the true literal sense. That honor was
reserved for what the text says about God or what I call the “God sense” of
a text.9
Theological interpretation was further buttressed by five core Reformed
beliefs about salvation, known as the five “solas” (Latin: alone): Sola Fide,
by faith alone (saved not by works); Sola Scriptura, by Scripture alone
(Scripture is interpreted by other passages in Scripture, against Roman
claims that the magisterium of the church has the final say in what texts
mean); Sola Gratia, by grace alone (we are justified not by merit); Solus
Christus, through Christ alone (the priest is not a mediator between the
individual and God); and Soli Deo Gloria, glory to God alone (not humans).
The Reformers understood that Scripture is to be read theologically with a
view to what it says about God and neighbor. Theological reading is the
church’s way to discover the Word.
Some critics might say that a law-gospel approach makes every text
conform to a predetermined pattern or template, or that it disregards
differences in biblical genres. Two responses may be given. First, every text
already implies both law and gospel, even if every preacher has not been
taught to recognize them. If preachers preach mainly trouble, as is common,

--- Page 127 ---
they inadvertently already unconsciously conform to a template, but one
that falls short of good news. Preachers may not even be aware that a bias
toward law and trouble governs their sermon content.
Second, consider this question: Do we preach the text or the gospel?
Hopefully both, not one or the other. Christ commissioned the church to “go
into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation” (Mark
16:15). He did not say, Go into the world and preach a text, or a unit of
Scripture, or a biblical concept; he said, Preach the gospel. To my mind that
means that we preach texts with a view to arriving at what is good about the
news.
Identifying God’s Action for the Theme Sentence
The Christian faith is not just abstract ideas or propositions; it is a
relationship with the Triune God, an experience, an event, a gift, a power in
the Spirit, and a motivating force for loving God and neighbor. Most books
on preaching speak of the need for the sermon to be about one idea, or what
many call a theme sentence. Even if a sermon has three points, these should
seem like horses pulling in one direction, not three. A theme sentence is one
of the key elements that give a sermon unity. It is the sermon in miniature
or microcosm. If the sermon is to get to the good news of God’s saving
action, the theme sentence needs to name that action decisively; otherwise
the sermon will remain mired in trouble and law. To preach good news is an
intentional decision guided by the Spirit.
Identifying a theme sentence is perhaps the single most important start in
sermon preparation for the preacher. If done on Monday, it provides
maximum time for mulling, reading, praying, and composing during the
week, and a maximum number of nights for the mind to problem-solve
during sleep. Once it is identified, the preacher can begin exegesis, consult
commentaries, and ask questions that get at relevant literary and historical
issues. The preacher interrogates the text, and the text also interrogates the
preacher and the contemporary situation.
At a 3-D movie theater, special glasses are needed to see all the
dimensions of the movie. In the same way, preachers need to put on special
glasses to see the dimensions of the good news. These glasses may be found
in the answer to one key question: What is God doing in or behind this text?
God’s action in the text is evident if one of the Persons of the Trinity is

--- Page 128 ---
mentioned. If not, God’s action behind the text in the larger section of
Scripture is sought. To be good news, these actions will be actions that
empower, forgive, reconcile, heal, guide, rescue, and so forth. The book of
Esther does not mention God, but both Esther and Mordecai are God’s
representatives, and their actions and words speak for God. The epistle of
James mostly says what people are to do yet simultaneously implies much
about our God, who mandates such behavior in response to faith. The good-
Samaritan text does not mention God yet affirms that God cares for the
outcast.
Law and gospel have a mirroring function. John Wesley once said,
“Every law contains a hidden promise.” By the same token, every message
of good news contains a hidden law. The dual nature of God’s Word
represents an important mirroring function. A text that seems like it is only
law, when read with the preacher’s special glasses, makes significant claims
about God’s love. “You shall not kill” also implies “God wills life.” A text
that seems to be only gospel implies trouble: “God finds the lost” also says
“Many are lost.”
This mirroring function of law and gospel is a principle of inversion.
Biblical genre is important; it provides important data about the text and its
meaning. Yet law and gospel are implied in nearly every text, regardless of
genre. One doctoral student chose to preach on the beheading of John the
Baptist in Matthew 14:1–12, a text that for most preachers would result in
only trouble. I feared the student might dwell on the violence in the text,
which in effect can be the same thing as preaching evil; the power of violent
images can be too hard to counter with good news. In fact, he used pastoral
sensitivity throughout. His theme sentence focused on what God is doing in
or behind the text: God prepared John the Baptist for his death. The sermon
explained that God allowed John to meet Jesus before he died. It was
brilliant.
Let us consider Romans 8:38–39, where Paul says that nothing “will be
able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.” A few
guidelines can be given for theme sentences in general:
1. Make God the subject, in one of the Persons of the Trinity. It might
be tempting to use “nothing” as the subject, as in “Nothing can
separate us from the love of God,” but the gospel comes into focus by
saying instead, “God holds us close no matter what.”

--- Page 129 ---
2. Choose an action of God that is saving, hopeful, or empowering (e.g.,
“holds us close”) to get to the gospel.
3. Use an action verb, if possible, to render God’s action more visible or
experiential, more concrete and less abstract. Use of a being verb like
“is” (as in “God is love”) may sometimes be necessary, but an action
verb makes it easier to visualize God’s action.
4. Ideally, keep the sentence short (four to six words), so you can repeat
it often in the sermon and listeners can easily recognize its
importance. It should be a single clause, not a complex sentence, and
not phrased as a question. The following is too long for hearers to
remember as a theme sentence: “God’s love for us in Jesus Christ is
so great that no matter what, God will keep us close.” It is
nonetheless a good argument to make in the sermon. It does not say
everything; it simply provides a focus for a fulsome exploration of
the text’s meaning.
5. Use a complete sentence to make an actual claim about the gospel,
not just announce a theme. “God’s love in Christ” is a theme but not
yet a sentence. It makes no claim.
In summary, the preacher’s first question early in the week can be, “What
is God’s action in or behind the text?” The theme sentence can be
determined even without the aid of biblical commentators, because if the
text does not mention God, they may not either. Commentators’ work is
essential, but it needs to be married to theological criticism. Theological
readings speak meaningfully, in text- and Spirit-directed ways, about God
and the gospel of Jesus Christ. Such readings necessarily invoke faith. From
the perspective of the pulpit, the church has always understood its readings
as theological, conforming to the regula fidei, not just to history or some
other standards.
Applicational Rationale
The homiletical term “application” normally refers to how a text applies to
our time and circumstances. In a law-gospel homiletic, many factors
contribute to application, including sermon arrangement and structure,
because there are at least two times of application.

--- Page 130 ---
Four Pages
If the sermon is in two parts, law and gospel (or trouble and grace), each
part needs to be applied to our time. We may thus identify what I call four
basic grammatical elements of the sermon, or Pages. They exist primarily as
a grammar, and secondarily as a possible form. A Page is not a literal page;
it is a metaphor for a quarter of the sermon. Page One is trouble in the
biblical text. Page Two is trouble in our world. If we were to stop here, we
would have the typical exposition/application sermon that has been the
bread-and-butter sermon of the church for many centuries. It is mostly
trouble—in other words, Pages One and Two. Page Three is grace in the
biblical text. It marks a return to the text, this time looking in or behind the
text for God’s saving action. Page Four is grace in our world. These Pages
are not a new invention. They represent basic grammatical elements that
have been part of preaching from the beginning. Augustine said that law
and grace are meanings to be found in every text of Scripture.10
Pages One and Three deal with the Bible as trouble and grace. And Pages
Two and Four deal with applications of the text to our world, as trouble and
grace. From a theological perspective, these four grammatical elements are
universal. Even preachers who do not know these elements still demonstrate
them in their preaching, though not necessarily in balanced ways. Apart
from material that is theologically neutral, what other options are there? No
matter what form a sermon may take, preachers speak about either the Bible
or today. And when they do, they speak about either human actions or
God’s. Any sermon can be analyzed using these four perspectives, whether
the preacher knew them or not. Weaknesses in preaching the gospel can be
traced to the absence of any one of the Pages. Most sermons in most
denominations are long on law and short on grace.
These Pages are not rigid grammatical rules; they are guidelines. Each
Page has its own focus, but other emphases can be mentioned in passing
(e.g., on Pages One and Two, grace can still be touched upon). Flexibility is
needed when using them as a form for preaching. Often preachers want to
start a sermon with a short account of a contemporary event. I tend to think
of that as an introduction prior to Page One. If the story is long, however, it
functions as a Page Two that leads into Page One.
Will the listeners find the Four Pages predictable week after week? Any
form can seem that way. Many preachers preach mostly law, and that can be
predictable. Four Pages as a grammar can be adapted to other forms, like a

--- Page 131 ---
sermon structured around three or more key points, where the first points
could be trouble and the later ones grace. Still, the Four Pages largely are
deep grammar, and most hearers simply listen at the surface level of
language. Even if the congregation was aware of the underlying movement
and grammar of Four Pages, they are not likely to say, “Oh, no. Now we are
going to hear good news.” That is what they came to hear. They need not
only instruction but also hope. A sermon is like a stained-glass window in
which the lead pieces, called “cames,” hold the glass together and give it
shape and structure. Viewers look not at the lead but at the colored glass.
Listeners receive Four Page sermons in the same way—they do not look at
the structure but for the beauty of the light that shines through.
Trouble (law) and grace (gospel) require between 50/50 and 60/40 of the
sermon time, given the need for introductory matters. Each must form in the
consciousness of the listener, and that takes time. The Pages can be
reordered. For instance, Pages could be sequenced: two, one, three, four, in
which case a preacher would begin with trouble in our times and in the
Bible, and move to grace in the Bible and our world. This three-stage
movement (our world/Bible [trouble + grace]/our world) can contribute to
easy listening and can be especially effective in shorter sermons. Or the
order could be One, Two, One, Two, Three, Four. The reader can list other
possible combinations. The longer the sermon, the more variations are
possible, but moving back and forth between Pages too often in a short
space of time can be overly demanding. The listeners must change all the
scenery in their minds with each major shift of location. A reversing
movement from grace back to trouble (i.e., from Page Three or Four back to
One or Two) is like a doctor stitching a wound and then unstitching it. I
suggest that preachers initially trying this approach as a form simply use the
sequence One, Two, Three, Four as a norm. When preachers try any new
approach, it is a good idea to try it not just once, but rather for an extended
period, like six months, as a kind of spiritual discipline, so that lessons
learned from it can be maximized. Six months is long enough for the
preacher to overlearn the approach, and for the effect on the congregation to
be noted.
Four Sentences

--- Page 132 ---
How is each Page to be determined? Each is governed by a short
sentence; thus, four simple sentences could be devised on Monday to
determine the entire sermon’s bare-bones structure. Start with the theme
sentence that governs Page Three (God’s saving action). In Luke 8:43–48
this could be, “Jesus heals the woman.” Then flip it using our principle of
inversion to determine Page One, “The woman needs healing.” The other
Pages fall into place as four sentences to guide composition:
Page One: The woman needs healing.
Page Two: We (or: Many) need healing.
Page Three: Jesus heals the woman.
Page Four: Jesus heals us (or: many).
In determining the four sentences, starting with Page Three and using
inversion to get to Page One ensures that the first and second halves of the
sermon fit like a hand and glove. Ask of the theme sentence: Why did God
act in this way? What was wrong in the biblical time? Once the four
sentences are in place, sermon composition can follow chronological order.
These four sentences provide just a skeleton of the Four Pages. They are
not the full meaning of the text, just a structure on which to develop it. They
might seem too simple, but simplicity is needed for a structural framework.
It provides a basic structure for the congregation to follow the preacher’s
line of thought. It will prompt more complex thoughts in actual
composition. Studies have shown that most people listen at a level of grade-
five education, even if they have been to university. We should speak so
that eleven-year-olds can understand. These sentences provide a handrail
for the preacher to remember the sermon and for the listeners to follow us
to the desired destination. The sentence that governs each Page needs to
appear in the sermon near the beginning of that Page.
Consider another example, Philippians 4:4–7. Preachers might
instinctively take as their theme sentence, “Rejoice in the Lord always”
(4:4). The problem is that the burden is on humans. It is trouble, not God’s
saving action. If we tell people to rejoice who are unable to do so on that
Sunday, we simply condemn them to their circumstances that week. A
deeper reading of the text yields the reason behind Paul’s instruction: “The
Lord is near.” (The verb is a being verb, but sometimes that is the best
word.) This is the theme sentence for Page Three. Page One by inversion
could be, “The people think that God is absent.” Here are four possible
sentences and some ideas for developing them:

--- Page 133 ---
Page One: The people think God is absent. Or, The people do not rejoice. To find trouble, the
preacher can read the entire passage (Phil. 4:4–7) by inversion. Paul prescribes things because
they are absent. The whole of Page One discusses this Bible text. Reference could also be made
to the “murmuring and arguing” in Philippians 2:14 and the “dogs” and “evil workers” in 3:2.
Page Two: We may think God is absent. Or, Some people find it hard to rejoice. I think of people
who have been dealt with unjustly or have suffered great loss. On this Page the preacher is not
tied to the biblical text itself, but seeks various connections with the wider world.
Page Three: The Lord is near. As with each of these four sentences, this idea should be at the
beginning of the Page, and the whole Page should be devoted to it as the theme sentence of the
sermon. Develop it with details from the text that support the gospel. Keep God as the subject of
many of the sentences. In other words, keep God’s actions central. Repeat the theme sentence. If
the preacher waits until the end of Page Three to get to the theme sentence, the result is another
Page One.
Page Four: Christ is present to us. When developing this Page, return to the people in the worst
situation mentioned on Page Two, in order that good news may be brought to them. For the
people present who mourn and cannot rejoice today, the preacher pronounces Christ’s blessing
to them right now, in this worship time, and the assurance of countless blessings in the past and
future, some of which can be named. A preacher might also cite texts like, “Blessed are those
who mourn” (Matt. 5:4), “[I] will wipe every tear from their eyes” (Rev. 21:4), and “The Father
. . . shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever” (John 14:16 KJV).
The four sentences are short. Be sure to repeat them in the sermon. They
are designed to be transitional sentences between the Pages. In the sermon,
they can be like trailblazing marks on trees for others to follow the path of
thought. Write one of the four sentences on each of four literal pages.
Compose each quarter of the sermon referring to that sentence. Each quarter
is about that one idea. Free-associate using it and discover new ideas,
stories, and connections. The four sentences both stimulate theological
creativity and keep the sermon on track, minimizing wasted hours and
disorganized effort. One can work on the sermon through the week in four
stages. These four sentences are like a tree at Christmas on which to hang
the essential ornaments of the faith—namely, information about the text,
church teachings, life experiences, and God’s abiding and sustaining love.
Sermon Unity
Elements of sermon unity contribute to how the sermon is applied to
today. Once composition is underway, the sermon can be tested for unity.
Unity can be ensured by having one Text, one Theme derived from that
text, one Doctrine derived from the theme, one Need also derived from the
theme, one Image linked to the theme, and one Mission that follows from

--- Page 134 ---
them. The initial letters TTDNIM can be used to compose a nonsensical
sentence to help jog the memory: The Tiny Dog Now Is Mine. Brief
explication is needed:
ONE TEXT
Normally choose only one biblical text to focus upon in depth. More than
one quickly becomes too demanding. The burning question of churchgoers
on any Sunday is not: How does the epistle connect with the Old Testament
lesson? They want a word of hope and reassurance applied to their lives.
They need to know that God is still in control of the world they see around
them. Other texts can be brought in as desired, as we just did above with
“Blessed are those who mourn,” and “[I] will wipe every tear from their
eyes,” but they are for passing reference, not in-depth exposition.
ONE THEME
We have already discussed the importance of a theme sentence on Page
Three. Partly by inversion, it governs and unifies the whole sermon,
preventing law and gospel from sounding like two separate sermons. To be
unifying, the theme sentence can be repeated many times in a sermon,
sometimes with variant wording. Each of the elements of unity, below, is
derived from the theme sentence.
ONE DOCTRINE
A doctrine is a teaching of the church, like Christology, soteriology,
pneumatology, eschatology, the nature of God, the nature of humans, sin,
redemption, and so forth. One solid doctrinal teaching emerging from the
theme sentence (or its inverse) applied to today in each sermon is a good
goal. I recommend that students identify a doctrine, and then read a few
pages about it in a volume of systematic or constructive theology. Such
disciplined practice over time can be like taking vitamins in small doses. It
can strengthen a preacher’s ability to reflect theologically and deepen
thought in each sermon. Sometimes the doctrine shifts in writing; for
example, a sermon that begins by talking about sin should end by talking
about redemption. Encouragement to have solid teaching from one doctrine
is nonetheless worthy.

--- Page 135 ---
ONE NEED
The sermon should speak to at least one person’s emotional, spiritual, or
physical need, without naming that person. The goal is a “felt need” that the
people might name for themselves, in their own words, not an intellectual
need, like “the people need to know more about the Trinity.” The
assumption here is that if one person’s need is met, many people’s needs
will be met. If a sermon does not meet the expressed need of anyone,
maybe it ought not to be preached.
Connecting at emotional and spiritual levels requires empathy. Preachers
should empathize with the worst person mentioned, because someone
present may identify with that person and that person needs God’s help. To
empathize means to show compassion for that person as a fellow human
being; it does not mean to condone or sympathize with what he or she may
have done by way of sin. The deepest need mentioned on Page One or Two
should be ministered to by the end of Page Four.
ONE IMAGE
One repeating or dominant image can help unify a sermon and ground it
in people’s experience. Any word picture is an image, and of course the
sermon should be as concrete as possible and use many sensory words. An
image becomes dominant, however, by its repetition in different sermon
locations, like the beginning, middle, and end. The image ideally comes
from the biblical text, like the treasure in clay jars, or the long bony finger
of John the Baptist pointing to Jesus as John says, “He must increase, but I
must decrease” (John 3:30). The image can be from our world, like a cat,
wheelbarrow, or snowflake, if it somehow links with the theme. It can be
words that summarize a story, like “summer vacation,” or “the long subway
ride,” or “the soaked baseball diamond,” or it can be a refrain like, “It’s
important to know what bus you are on.”
ONE MISSION
Different writers speak about what they want the congregation to do
because of hearing a sermon. I call mission what Henry Mitchell calls a
behavioral purpose and what Tom Long calls a function statement. God’s
Word calls hearers to act. The mission may be something everyone needs to
do, like forgive, yet everyone’s mission is not necessarily the same on any

--- Page 136 ---
given Sunday. One person may be called to serve in the food kitchen,
another may be called to teach Sunday school, someone else may need to
stop by Mrs. Ellsworth’s home to feed her goldfish while she is in the
hospital. Preachers may suggest a few mission possibilities in hope that the
Spirit will direct individuals to claim their own calling.
Mission is what we are required to do, so it is a dimension of law and it
belongs on Page Two. But mission also belongs at the end of the sermon,
and here a problem arises. The sermon is devoted in a sense to changing the
water of daily life into the wine of faithful living, to moving the people
from trusting in their own resources to trusting in God. If mission comes
toward the end, does it not change the wine back into water?
My understanding is that the proclaimed gospel transforms not only
individuals, communities, and situations but also the nature of mission. In
Mark 16:1–8, the “young man, dressed in a white robe,” instructs the
women on Easter morning to tell the disciples that Jesus has gone ahead of
them into Galilee; he concludes, “There you will see him” (8:7). Galilee is
the place of their mission and ours. In effect the disciples are told that in
going where they are sent, they will meet the risen Christ. I like to think that
our own missions are invitations to meet the resurrected Christ, who has
gone ahead of us into places of need. Mission can be not just duty (Page
Two) but an invitation to empowerment and renewal (Page Four). The
gospel message transforms all of the “musts, shoulds, have-tos, and oughts”
on Page Two into “you may now, there is nothing that can prevent you, God
has prepared the way for you to . . .” On Page Four, mission strikes the
hearer as good news, or at the very least, as Calvin’s promise of sweetness
and excitement to obedience.
Application on Pages Two and Four
Application is mostly centered on Pages Two and Four. Here is a sermon
sketch based on 2 Corinthians 4:1–15, regarding the treasure in clay jars.
The theme sentence is, “God makes Christ’s resurrection visible in the
Corinthians.”
Page One: Paul’s people suffer in many ways. Paul refers to brokenness and sin in his
community in Corinth, to people who are unable to see the light of Christ. Describe his Corinth.
Use narrative as much as possible to reconstruct the biblical text and its background. Possibly
refer to the earlier suffering of Paul and his people from his previous visit (2:1–4). Instead of
writing an essay, try to conceive of the sermon as making a movie with words. Be imaginative

--- Page 137 ---
without distorting the text. Picture Paul writing his letter, perhaps looking out the window and
seeing a woman carrying a clay jar on her head, and it falls, scattering the contents. It leads him
to think of the Corinthians and their treasure of faith in clay jars. The clay jars can serve as a
dominant image. It points to the power of God that can be more apparent in frailty of the body.
Page Two: We (or, Many people) suffer in many ways. Here there is no need to confine the
application to the church today, just because Paul discusses suffering in the Corinthian church.
The preacher is both theologian and poet-in-residence, and is free on Page Two to identify any
individual or communal situations that need Christ’s light. Suffering comes not just because
people directly violate God’s Word (trouble on a vertical axis). It comes also because we live in
a fallen world (trouble on a horizontal axis) with the consequences of the world being not as
God intended. The social situations around us are of concern to God, like poverty, racism, and
injustice. Highlighting a key story from the news can be a way to keep the sermon vital. God
alone offers what is needed. Try not to confine the application to just the local area; go national
and global to teach that all people are our neighbors and that God provides and cares for all
people in need. Tell the story of a family you saw interviewed on the news; describe and quote
them. In so doing you make the people come to life for hearers and you make the stories
personal. By allowing listeners to meet people in need through the sermon, you avoid letting
social issues remain abstract, and God’s help can be seen concretely. Seek a balance between
abstract analysis and narrative accounts.
Page Three (theme sentence): God makes Christ’s resurrection visible in the Corinthians (see
v. 10). For the theme sentence, a preacher might get hooked by a vital image and instinctively
opt for, “We have our treasure in clay jars,” but that is not the good news. Don’t preach the
image, preach the core good-news idea behind it, and use the image to serve it. Preaching an
image can fire up our creativity like a grill on July 4th, without anything to put on the grill. Even
though this is Page Three, brief reference could be made to Leonard Cohen’s “Anthem”: “Forget
your perfect offering. / There’s a crack in everything. / That’s how the light gets in.”
Page Four: God makes Christ’s resurrection visible in us. In many ways, this is the hardest Page
to write in any sermon and may need the most composition time, in part because great care is
needed to faithfully represent God in the world. It is relatively easy to find stories or situations
of law or trouble for Page Two. Here, however, we need stories with sufficient weight that point
to God acting, often through our own actions or the actions of others. In faith we make claims
on behalf of God led by the Spirit.
Still, there should be no shortage of situations to which we can point.
Simply look for signs of the new creation or signs of the realm or kingdom
of God breaking into our midst. Such signs include mercy, justice, peace,
homes for the homeless, food for the hungry, comfort for the mourning,
healing, love, forgiveness, righteousness—the list goes on. In the case of
2 Corinthians 4:1–15 and fragile clay jars, we might point to a story of
someone who has little by way of health, wealth, or power, yet who inspires
much. Such ministry could be claimed as an expression of Christ’s
resurrection and his ongoing multiplication of the loaves and fishes.
Preachers often make the mistake of thinking that stories for Page Four
need to be about religious people, like Mother Teresa. A few problems exist

--- Page 138 ---
with this kind of thinking: (1) Such stories are good, but we quickly run out
of them. (2) Listeners often convert stories of amazing people into trouble
when applying them to themselves: “I could never do that.” (3) We do not
need to look only far afield to find amazing people and God’s actions.
(4) Whether or not people in a story are religious, God might still act
through them.
How we preachers tell stories can be what makes them religious. Here is
the key: Tell the story of someone’s good works and then do what most
preachers omit doing. Point to God’s signature in the lower corner of the
picture. Claim any good action as God’s. By attributing all goodness to
God, we change a story from something congregants must imitate
(= trouble) into a story of possibility. The same God empowers each of us to
fulfill our own calling. This is a message of hope listeners need to hear.
Application and Elements of Unity for 2 Corinthians 4:1–15
The elements in “The Tiny Dog Now Is Mine” help establish sermon
unity, but they also have relevance for application:
One Text: Other Bible texts connect with the frail vessels that bear our faith, like Isaiah 42:3: “A
bruised reed he will not break, and a dimly burning wick he will not quench; he will faithfully
bring forth justice.” Such texts can help apply the message, without being explored in depth.
One Theme: God makes Christ’s resurrection visible in the Corinthians.
One Doctrine: From the theme sentence an obvious teaching of the church is the doctrine of
ministry (i.e., Christ is evident through our service to others). Other doctrines could be
revelation (i.e., God reveals Christ to others) or resurrection (i.e., we participate in Christ’s
resurrection life now). Choose one. Such teachings may be brought into the sermon on any
Page.
One Need: Who needs to hear the theme sentence, “God makes Christ’s resurrection visible in
the Corinthians”? Perhaps it is someone who thinks God is remote on this Sunday, or people
who have received terrible news. Speak about them on Page Two. On Page Four return with
encouraging good news from God.
One Image: Treasure in clay jars—even when what we have seems fragile, God can work
powerful things through us.
One Mission: Serve others and discover Christ’s empowerment.
Proclamation

--- Page 139 ---
Proclamation is the most intimate form of application. I have devoted an
entire volume to this topic in my Setting Words on Fire: Putting God at the
Center of the Sermon,11 and here I will give only a brief overview.
“Proclamation” is commonly understood to be a synonym for “preaching,”
but here and in the history of preaching it has a more refined meaning. It is
one of two dimensions of preaching: teaching and proclamation.
Teaching tells us about certain things like the Bible, history, theology,
and God. Teaching is essential in a sermon. It communicates vital
information. It forms faithful followers of Christ. At the same time,
communicating information is not primary in preaching. Nor is solving a
problem. Some people mistakenly view Four Pages as problem/solution, but
it is not, unless we characterize crucifixion/resurrection that way. We do not
preach people into a solution to their problems; we preach them into a
relationship with the living Christ. That is primary. As a result of that
relationship, problems will be solved. That is important but secondary.
Preaching is relational ahead of being informational or solution driven. We
preach the identity, will, and transforming action of God, and therefore the
identity, actions, and hopes of empowered disciples.
If you are on a tour of an estate in England, it is one thing to be taught
about the baroness who owns it, and it is quite another thing to meet her in
person. Proclamation, in addition to teaching, allows the listeners not just to
hear about God, but to meet God. Proclamation is direct first-to-second-
person (I-to-you) speech, direct from God to the people. It bears God’s
saving power. Isaiah 55:11 says, “So shall my word be that goes out from
my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that
which I purpose, and succeed in the thing for which I sent it.” That
understanding is particularly apt for proclamation.
The people should be clear in hearing proclamation that God is speaking.
On Page Two, proclamation can come as law or trouble: “Change your
ways. Your path leads to death.” On Page Four it is the most intimate kind
of speech possible: “I love you. I forgive you. You are mine. I will be with
you. I will wipe away every tear from your eyes.” And so on. In the light of
the biblical text, the words one places in God’s mouth are the words of love,
encouragement, healing, and liberation that the preacher, in faith,
understands that God would say to the people directly if God were
preaching (which on one level is in fact the case). In proclamation, the
preacher steps aside for God to “do the gospel” to the people. Of course,

--- Page 140 ---
proclamation is not just up to the preacher. The preacher listens to what the
Spirit is saying. People can hear the intimate renewing words of
proclamation even through an artless sermon if the Spirit wills.
Proclamation can be misused and abused. Preachers can put terrible
words into God’s mouth, which is a caution for any form of preaching.
Alternatively, some preachers might think that to speak for God is too bold,
forgetting that that is what they do or are heard to do every time they
preach. If the preacher is not clear, some people in the pews might hear the
words, “I love you. You are mine,” and mistakenly think them to be from
the preacher and find that creepy. Still others may find in proclamation a
claim of vertical authority that has often marked tragic abuses in the church.
We need to be mindful that people today tend to mistrust people who lack
transparency and accountability.
Proclamation at the beginning of a sermon is empty or facile, but when it
comes later in the sermon, emerging out of solid teaching, it comes as
rescue for the dying and hope for the hopeless. It is a saving word direct
from God. It is sacramental, it offers Christ to the people, thus it requires
the highest degree of sensitivity and care.
We have discussed a balanced sermonic movement from law/trouble to
gospel/grace. The purpose has been to preach the gospel. We need not
merely communicate information about God; we may communicate Christ,
who encounters us in the Word, as Paul says, “with a demonstration of the
Spirit and of power” (1 Cor. 2:2–4). That power is Christ’s victory over all
death.

Response to Paul Scott Wilson
BRYAN CHAPELL
The decades of respected work from Paul Scott Wilson are well
summarized in his chapter on the law-gospel view. He writes with his
typical clarity to help evangelicals outside circles where the law-gospel
paradigm is most frequently employed to understand the concepts that
guide preachers in this tradition. Further, he writes with appropriate nuance
to undermine the stereotype that all sermons employing this hermeneutic
simply divide into law = bad / gospel = good segments.
His clarification of what constitutes law is particularly helpful and is
written with the honest assessment that “law and gospel” are loaded terms. I
doubt if he and I in this brief interchange will add much to the ongoing
discussion among Protestant traditions regarding who has the best
formulation of the law. Rather it is helpful in using him as a conversation
partner to answer critics who sometimes mistakenly characterize Christ-
centered preaching as reconstituted “Lutheran” preaching (since, as Wilson
notes, the law-gospel hermeneutic is most frequently associated with
Lutheranism).
In his biblical and theological rationales for analyzing texts, Wilson
briefly describes the historic differences in the Lutheran, Calvinistic, and
Wesleyan formulations of the law, and then proceeds to identify how these
formulations affect his preaching. His distinctions help nuance the
differences between a Reformed preacher doing redemptive preaching and
preachers from other traditions doing law-gospel preaching.
Though he says that “‘law’ and ‘gospel’ are not binary terms, like ‘bad’
and ‘good’” (121), and that “both law and gospel are needed” (121), his
preaching methodology approaches the categories as “trouble and grace.”
By categorizing the law as “trouble,” trouble that must be countered or
solved by grace in subsequent “Pages” of the sermon, Wilson maintains his
law-gospel hermeneutic at the same moment that he separates from the
Reformed understanding.

--- Page 143 ---
Most Protestant traditions agree on the necessity of the law restraining
evil and revealing human weakness. But the added Reformed emphasis is
that the law is a reflection of the character and care of God. By the moral
standards of the law God establishes a good and safe path for us to journey
through a fallen world. In this sense, the law is an expression of the grace of
God.
Our inability to stay on the path reveals to us our fallen nature and drives
us to the cross, but that does not mean that the law is not gracious. Wilson’s
nuanced description of the law makes this point also, but it is important that
critics of redemptive-historical preaching know that the stereotype of the
“grace guys” dispensing with the law “like the Lutherans” fits neither our
principles nor theirs.
What may seem to reinforce the stereotype is a common Lutheran
approach (echoed by Wilson) of beginning sermons with the law and, then,
countering that portion of the message with a later explanation of “the
gospel.” Inevitably this creates the impression that the gospel is supplanting
the law in some way. The impression is strong enough that Wilson writes
emphatically, “The law is not a straw argument made irrelevant by the
subsequent gospel” (121).
One of the things that I love about Wilson’s approach is his clarity
regarding the necessity of revering the gospel and identifying the ways that
sermons can sound biblical and still not “get to God.” His analysis of how
sermons can “get to grace” and still err is a treasure. His four-page solution
to making sure the gospel is not neglected is the fruit of Wilson’s many
years of expert instruction and is very helpful.
The downside of the four-page approach is that its first two pages still
reflect on the law primarily as “trouble” (in the biblical text first, and then
in our world). Although Wilson says clearly, “The trouble-to-grace
paradigm is not to be confused with problem/solution” (127), the approach
may tip listeners toward that misimpression. Those who are familiar with
my approach of first identifying the “fallen condition” in the text and then
identifying how that condition reflects in our world may think I am being
inconsistent by surfacing this concern, but the difference in approach can be
significant.
The fallen condition focus is a statement of the flaw or weakness in our
observance of the law or handling our world, not of the “trouble” of the law.
I well recognize that preachers trained by Wilson and others with similar

--- Page 144 ---
law-gospel perspectives would not intentionally undermine the moral
standards of the law (though the acceptance of the critical lenses he
identifies may do so). However, the consistent sermon sequence that makes
the gospel the counterbalance to the “trouble” of the law can seem to
devalue the standards we all wish to honor.
Care to identify the fallen condition that is the focus of specific pericopes
may also help with another concern that Wilson identifies with the
regularity of his four-page approach—that is, every sermon seeming to
sound the same. If the fallen condition is identified early in the message, the
preacher may have more options for unfolding gospel implications:
(1) laying an early foundation of grace that is the basis for handling the
fallen condition, (2) interweaving grace dynamics through the sermon that
deal with the different dimensions of the fallen condition, (3) leading to an
understanding of how grace is the answer to the fallen condition developed
through the message, (4) showing how grace provides motivation and/or
enabling for applications that address the fallen condition, or
(5) combinations of all of these.
I can readily identify with Wilson in having to face critics who say,
“Every sermon will sound the same if we have to talk about grace every
time,” and I am his greatest ally on this front. Hardly anyone says, “Our
sermons will sound the same if we have to talk about law every time,”
because they know there are so many dimensions to the law of God. What
the fallen condition approach allows is for preachers to keep turning the
diamond of grace to the different dimensions of the human dilemma, so that
grace is as rich and varied as the human fallenness Scripture addresses is
poor and diverse.
Further, by identifying the fallen condition the grace of God addresses,
we avoid getting caught in the caricature so often drawn of preachers who
consistently excavate redemptive truth from the span and scope of Scripture
(i.e., “You identify the law, then tell people they can’t really do it, but Jesus
will forgive them anyway”). Rather, we say, “This is what the law requires.
It consequently reveals your fallenness, which requires the grace of God;
but, in the power of that grace, you can (and should) follow the good and
safe path that God has designed for his people.”

Response to Paul Scott Wilson
ABRAHAM KURUVILLA
Paul Scott Wilson, a senior statesman of preaching, is always worth
reading. His thoughtful approach to homiletics has benefited Christendom
for decades. May that continue!
As I had mentioned, I am following a somewhat random, eyewear-
themed outline. Chapell’s “gospel glasses” and Langley’s “lens” I
considered earlier. Wilson’s “bifocals” will be addressed here. In my essay,
I did not employ any such optic device. Either I am completely blind or
have perfect vision. But I confess that my optically challenged colleagues
have a point: every interpreter—and that includes me—has a particular
scheme of interpretation (vision-embellishing accoutrement—“glasses”—of
some sort).
Wilson’s ophthalmic equipment enables him to see the “bifocal” nature
of Scripture as it deals with sin and salvation, and so he, like Chapell,
asserts that “the purpose of preaching is to proclaim the gospel” (117). In
every sermon? As a hermeneutical constraint of every text? Apparently so,
for Wilson uses Mark 16:15 to assert that Christ commissioned the church
to proclaim the “good news,” not “preach a text, or a unit of Scripture, or a
biblical concept” (129). Thus, he makes the variegated texts of Scripture
monomorphic, compelling them to serve the monolithic function of gospel
presentations (employing bifocals or a monocle?). Ignoring the thorny issue
of Markan endings, one sees that the particular context of 16:14–16 deals
with salvation, so, of course, the gospel must be proclaimed! But what
about the parallel in Matthew 28:19–20, which reports Jesus commissioning
his followers to make disciples by baptizing (the consequence of
justification) and “teaching them to observe all that I commanded you” (the
course of sanctification)? There is more to the life of a child of God than
being born; there is also growth and conformity to Christlikeness. In fact,
the bulk of Scripture speaks to this latter aspect, almost all of its pericopes
being directed to those already in relationship with God. I would argue,
therefore, that the proper background of every text is God’s conformation of

--- Page 147 ---
his children to Christlikeness (Rom. 8:29), as he consummates all things in
the cosmos in Christ (Eph. 1:9–10). With this grand terminus in view, it is
christiconic preaching that sets the pericopes of Scripture in their proper
context—the magnificent and glorious plan of God, vast in space and, in
time, eternal.1
As did Chapell’s terminology, Wilson’s labels and his sermonic
“sentences” also tend to obscure our vision. For instance, confessing that
“‘law’ and ‘gospel’ are not always easy terms” (121), he prefers “trouble”
and “grace.” But why is God’s call for holiness (“law”) “trouble” and
“basically anthropocentric” (122)? Then there are the sentences that govern
each “Page” of Wilson’s sermon structures. Preachers apparently are to use
those sentence reductions (of an irreducible text!) to compose the sermon.
Craddock was right about such distilling industries: “The minister boils off
all the water [the text] and then preaches the stain in the bottom of the cup
[reduced sentences]”2—a stain then decorated by one’s fancies, as one
decks “a tree at Christmas” with “ornaments”!
Wilson’s exploration of the Lukan account of the hemorrhaging woman
(8:43–48) caught my attention. In the first place, the pericope is incomplete
without the “sandwiching” outer story—the narrative of Jairus’s daughter
and her restoration to life (8:40–42, 49–56). Secondly, the reading is
alarmingly “thin,” with no apparent attempt made to catch the author’s
doing in that intricate narrative pericope. The story is simply reduced to
four sentences with a first-person-plural pronoun mechanically substituted
for “woman”: The woman needs healing. We need healing. Jesus heals the
woman. Jesus heals us. No mention of “faith” or “believe” (8:48, 50)? Or of
the two “daughters” in the story, one dying at age twelve, the other
suffering for twelve years? And how all of this (and other textual details)
contributes to the author’s doing? The spectacular color spectrum of the
pericope is reduced to a blurry black-and-white photocopy.3
This color blindness is another pernicious interpretive affliction of
epidemic proportions. Careful (“thick”) readings of texts for application,
discerning the doings of biblical authors, are virtually nonexistent these
days; even Bible translators seem unaware of the importance of pragmatics
(see my example from 1 Sam. 15). The need of the hour is, therefore,
painstaking theological exegesis that discerns the theology of the pericope
from the textual clues therein. This alone leads to valid application.4 In the
absence of such exegesis, without comprehending what the author is doing

--- Page 148 ---
with what he is saying, preachers have no recourse but to resort to New
Testament allusions, crucicentric apologetics, theological abstractions, and
inutile atomizations—none of which has much to do with the thrust of a
particular text. Blind to the color (i.e., doings) of the text, we resort to other
modalities (“glasses”) of interpretation. But eyewear is futile when the
malady is in the occipital cortex.
The focus of a sermon must be on discerning the theology of the pericope
and deriving application. Indeed, even the basic elements of my christiconic
interpretation need not be recited in each preaching event. At most, a one-
or two-minute statement of the importance of conformation to Christ would
be sufficient in each sermon. That also goes for the related idea of God’s
grace—in Christ and through the Spirit—which empowers believers to
obey. This could be achieved even in a prayer following the sermon, à la
Augustine: “Father, what you call us to do in this text, we are utterly
incapable of accomplishing with our own contrivances and devices. Grant,
through your Holy Spirit, the wherewithal to accomplish what you would
want us to, that we may be like your Son, for the sake of your glory” (my
loose paraphrase of Confessions 10.29.40).
So, while acknowledging that I too have eyewear, I would nonetheless
assert that I do not expansively expound my optic gear in every sermon
(contacts, not immediately apparent to others?). I would rather use my
precious in-the-pulpit minutes to focus on the uniqueness and specificity of
each pericope preached than rehash weekly the unchanging items of my
christiconic hermeneutic. One should not expect the minuscule sliver of
time that is the Sunday sermon to do everything. My plea is that the sermon
in a worship service rather be dedicated to discerning the theology of each
pericope from specific textual clues and deriving powerful application that
progressively molds believers into the image of Christ in the power of the
Spirit. I fear we have misunderstood what the sermon is all about and whom
it is for.
Privileging of the allotted text for the day to discern the theology of that
pericope—what the author is doing with what he is saying—is,
unfortunately, hardly ever described, taught, or modeled in classes,
conferences, books, or journals (forget pulpits). This lacuna leaves
preachers visionless, struggling to figure out how to handle a given
pericope. Preaching has for too long been ruled by linguists and rhetoricians
with a consuming focus on parsing, diagramming, outlining, illustrating,

--- Page 149 ---
delivering, and so on—all of it useful, no doubt. But unless one arrives at
the text’s pragmatics and its author’s doings, every other exertion is
ultimately effete, as far as valid application, life change, and conformity to
Christ are concerned. It is time biblical hermeneutics incorporating
pragmatics took center stage in homiletics to privilege the text.
Let me again thank the editors and my colleagues for the opportunity to
engage in this scripted dialogue. “For now we see in a mirror, dimly,” but
one day we shall be glassless, disgoggled, bifocal-free, and de-contact-ed.
Until that bright day, forward!

Response to Paul Scott Wilson
KENNETH LANGLEY
I’ve read Paul Scott Wilson for years and never without profit. It’s a
privilege to partner with this prolific homiletician in our current project.
I concur wholeheartedly with his chapter’s statements about looking for
the action of God in every text and naming God in the sermon’s key
sentences. He and I are on the same page insofar as he advocates a
theocentric focus.
I like his suggestion that preachers point to God’s signature in any picture
of good that someone does, so that the illustration implies not an obligation
to imitate a human example so much as God-empowering possibility. I’m
going to try to do this more often in my own preaching.
I agree with his counsel on sermon unity, beginning with the
recommendation to exposit one text. It’s not only lectionary preachers who
might be inclined to pull together multiple passages in one sermon; the
sermons I heard as a youngster in a fundamental Baptist church had
listeners flipping pages every few minutes as we compared scripture with
scripture, looking at maybe ten or more but not looking at any one of them
long enough to really see what was there.
I also appreciate the care Wilson takes explaining the “dynamic, tensive
relationship” of law and gospel (124). This helps preachers like me who do
not entirely share his homiletical philosophy to still use profitably the law-
gospel lens to better understand many biblical texts. We’ll realize, for
example, that even the most scathing prophetic oracles can be indirect
witnesses to grace, for God was under no obligation but love to warn and
plead with lawbreakers before executing judgment.
Wilson invites us to think of the “Four Pages of the Sermon” as deep
grammar and not a rigid rule. However, when he speaks of shuffling these
pages in different orders and assigns an ideal percentage range to the
amount of airtime they get, it’s clear he expects trouble in the text, trouble
in our world, grace in the text, and grace in our world to structure every
sermon. Wilson can be commended for providing beginning preachers with

--- Page 151 ---
a flexible, learnable model and for making explicit the link between his
theology and his homiletical method.
But why not let the theology of each pericope determine the amount of
pulpit time spent on trouble and grace (if indeed trouble and grace are the
best way of understanding what that particular text is saying), and why not
let the form of the text determine the order? For example, most psalms of
lament make a turn to confidence at some point, a satisfying trouble-then-
grace pattern, but the relative amount of complaint and praise varies
considerably from psalm to psalm, and some psalms (e.g., 44, 89) reverse
the pattern, first recounting God’s faithfulness in the past and then asking,
“Where are you now? Why do you stand by while our enemies prevail?”
Shouldn’t the preacher respect this textual movement in the sermon instead
of importing a movement that works well with many other texts? There
may be psychological or pastoral reasons for not following the order of a
text in the development of some sermons, but these occasions should be
exceptions. Part of the total package of a text is how it says what it says; I’d
rather take my sermon structure from that than from a one-size-fits-all
theological construct. Following the movement of the text will mean that
sometimes the sermon stresses trouble (say, 70/30 or 80/20), and may even
end there; perhaps that’s what the church needs this week. In a culture like
ours, where antinomianism is at least as big a threat as legalism, a bit more
attention to law/trouble might be in order.
Not only does letting the form of the text shape the form of the sermon
seem more respectful of the human author and the divine Author; it also
makes for natural variety in sermons: no preacher will have to resort to
gimmicks or the latest homiletic du jour for variety. The Bible’s own richly
varied genres and pericopal theology will do quite nicely.
But perhaps we should preach the gospel, not texts? Wilson says both:
and he doesn’t mean texts some weeks and the gospel other weeks, but both
every week. I’m not so sure, and citing the great commission doesn’t settle
the matter. If “gospel” is defined broadly enough, then of course every act
of Christian proclamation should be gospel. If gospel is limited to Paul’s
“Christ died, Christ arose,” or to the apostolic kerygma in the speeches of
Acts, then I think we’d all agree that pastors, at least (if not full-time
evangelists), must give congregations a great deal more: “anything,” as Paul
put it in his farewell to the Ephesian elders, “that would be helpful to you”
(Acts 20:20, 27).1 Fluid New Testament vocabulary for proclamation does

--- Page 152 ---
not allow us to limit “preaching” to evangelizing the unconverted and to
limit “teaching” to instructing believers. Nor do I lose my identity as a
preacher of the gospel if on some Sundays grace is less explicit or if the text
at hand gives needed warning, exhortation, or instruction or takes my
people and me into creation and wisdom themes.
The text at hand will always take us to God! Whether or not we always
encounter trouble or grace in a text, we will—or should—always encounter
God. In his 2004 Preaching and Homiletical Theory, Wilson wrote, “A
paradigm shift is needed in homiletics as a whole to return God to the
center of Scripture.”2 I say, “Amen!”

Conclusion
SCOTT M. GIBSON AND MATTHEW D. KIM
We are grateful to the authors for their contributions to this volume and
their willingness to engage with each other as we wrestle with the various
approaches to preaching. In this concluding chapter, we will consider areas
of agreement among the authors, explain the problem with lenses, discuss
the compelling elements of each approach, and raise some matters for
consideration.
Areas of Agreement and the Problem with Lenses
We recognize that each author takes Scripture seriously and is passionate
about the Word of God being preached faithfully. However, we note that
each author reads the text with a theological framework and makes a
theological commitment in his exegesis and preaching. Each of the
preachers considers scriptural application to be of great significance to the
task of preaching. Additionally, each author agrees that preaching is
difficult. One of the difficulties all admit is the task of moving from the past
to the present.
While there are areas of agreement, we observe limitations to one’s
particular hermeneutical lens. Lenses are inevitable and shaped by our
gender, race, ethnicity, culture, socioeconomic and educational
backgrounds, experiences, denominational affiliation, seminary training or
lack thereof, and so on, and all authors agree that they wear a particular lens
when engaged in exegesis.
One of the questions we want to raise with the authors and our readers is
this: Is there a pure hermeneutical form that can take the text at face value
without imposing one’s theological framework? Is this even possible?
Another question to the authors is, How can one read Scripture and enter
the biblical world since we are approaching the text with our particular
lens? Lenses have different colors/hues (that allow you to see some things

--- Page 154 ---
and not others), prescriptions (are nearsighted/farsighted), materials (glass
or plastic). We discovered that each preacher has a slightly different
emphasis on the purpose of preaching, an emphasis determined by the lens
that they use. With this in mind, one might sense that lenses are
individualized so that the prescription of one’s lenses is often individualized
and not applied to the corporate body.
Observations on Hermeneutics
Hermeneutics drives preaching. The contributors to this volume approach
the task of preaching from a set hermeneutical position. In this next section,
we will discuss the compelling elements of their hermeneutics and provide
some issues for consideration.
Compelling Elements
Chapell: Preaching grace/Christ as a solution for our sin and motivation
for our obedience is an attractive solution for our sin problem. Chapell
gives us a biblical ethic for what motivates sanctification and holiness.
Chapell recognizes that listeners today need to hear about the grace of God
regularly. Chapell provides a helpful grid to understand how “every text
stands in relation to Christ’s ministry” (7), some texts being predictive of
the work of Christ, others preparatory for the work of Christ, and others
reflective of the work of Christ and/or resultant from the work of Christ.
Kuruvilla: We appreciate that Kuruvilla’s hermeneutic distinguishes what
the author was doing from what he’s saying (semantics and pragmatics).
His focus on what the scriptural author was doing provides a natural
connection to life application. Kuruvilla’s image of the “world in front of
the text” provides a hopeful response to the text and what life should be like
in the kingdom of God. Kuruvilla helps us to appreciate the importance of
pericopal theology in allowing the text of the sermon to speak for itself.
Langley: Langley helps us to recognize the importance of God’s work in
Scripture, while not focusing only on one member of the Godhead. Yet he
affirms that the Bible is the story of God’s revelation demonstrated and
fulfilled in Christ through the power of the Holy Spirit. Langley helpfully
reminds us that in all things Christ and the Holy Spirit submit to the will of
God the Father.

--- Page 155 ---
Wilson: Wilson sees preaching as the proclaiming of the gospel. He
shows us a helpful distinction by asking us to see the Word in terms of
law/trouble and gospel/grace. These are helpful distinctions. However, they
may not capture the breadth of what a passage may be conveying. Although
Wilson clarifies for us that “‘law’ and ‘gospel’ are not binary terms” (121),
to put each passage into an either/or theological tension seems to limit the
work of the text.
Matters for Consideration
Chapell: One issue for consideration concerns Chapell’s recommendation
that the preacher ask of every text, “What does this text reflect of human
nature that requires redemption?” (16). This question may seem
individualistic, leaving out the larger listening community, possibly the
immediate biblical context, and the situation of the biblical author and
world. One wonders if preachers really need to “disclose where every text
stands in relation to Christ’s ministry” (7). This statement raises the
question of the purpose of Scripture. Certainly, understanding the relation
of a biblical text to Christ’s ministry is one way to interpret Scripture, but is
it the only or primary way? Additionally, is redemption the point, purpose,
and goal of every text? Chapell notes, “Jesus is the apex and culmination of
Scripture’s testimony” (9), citing John Calvin. Paul affirms this and seems
to push it further when he writes that God has “highly exalted” Christ, so
that “every tongue [should] acknowledge that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the
glory of God the Father” (Phil. 2:11 NIV), recognizing the place of the
Father in redemption. Even though Chapell cautions preachers on typology,
there yet appears to be a danger of typology, allegory, and spiritualizing the
passage, without clear guidelines. As Chapell observes, “As the movement
has matured and its preachers have multiplied” (18), there appear to be
many ways to engage in Christ-centered preaching, but these many ways
raise the question about a consistent, reliable approach to doing it.
Kuruvilla: A more developed definition of the term “christiconic” would
be helpful. Additionally, what does the “world in front of the text” mean?
Ideal world, yes, but specifically, what does this look like? Further, a
critique of Ricoeur’s “world in front of the text” would be helpful.
Kuruvilla suggests that when interpreting the Bible, “we interpret the text
for preaching (and life change)” (50). This statement raises questions about

--- Page 156 ---
the thrust of the text and the function(s) of Scripture. How do we know
what the author was doing with what he was saying? Is there a method for
preachers to follow to determine what the author was doing with what he
was saying? Do all texts provide a “christiconic” image or characteristic of
Christ, so that that must be read into the text?
Langley: When Langley writes “God,” does he mean Father, Son, and
Holy Spirit, or specifically God the Father? While he argues that preaching
should be theocentric, one wonders if preaching a God-centered
hermeneutic and homiletic may not permit enough sermonic space for
trinitarian understandings of preaching. The lack of specificity of what
God-centered preaching means (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit?) raises the
question about the role of the Holy Spirit in hermeneutics for preaching.
The corollary of this is that listeners may be left wondering about the place
of the Holy Spirit.
Wilson: Further definition of the term “trouble” as it relates to “law”
would be helpful. The commitment to an interpretive lens (law-gospel) for
each passage may not always seem to fit the prescribed Four Pages,
although grace might be implied in the law. One wonders how to safeguard
trouble/law from becoming moralistic preaching or from moving too
quickly to gospel-grace. Additionally, one might judge that the binary of
law-trouble and gospel-grace is too rigid to allow Scripture to speak for
itself, similar to the limitations of the other hermeneutical approaches in
this book.
Observations on Homiletics
Homiletics arises out of hermeneutics. The contributors to this volume draw
from their hermeneutical positions and develop sermons accordingly. In this
next section, we will discuss the compelling elements of their homiletics
and then provide issues for consideration.
Compelling Elements
Chapell: He is correct in asserting that people need to hear about the
grace of God in Christ Jesus. It is important to hear the metanarrative
(historic-redemptive) of Scripture regularly. Additionally, Chapell provides
a helpful caution against preaching moralism.

--- Page 157 ---
Kuruvilla: The emphasis on discipleship and sanctification of believers is
a helpful and compelling motivation for preaching. Kuruvilla’s advice is
helpful, that being “aligned to God’s specific will in that particular
pericope” (62) will provide the preacher with guidance for application of
the text. Kuruvilla is gifted in retelling the narrative in light of what the
author was doing with what he was saying.
Langley: The Bible is the story of God. God-centered preaching may be a
clearer way to preach to nonbelievers who may not understand the
complexities of the Trinity. Langley suggests a homiletical template that
preachers can easily follow. He thoughtfully considers how a preacher
might determine the parameters of interpreting a text in light of its Christ-
connectedness.
Wilson: A clear structure for preaching is provided by the Four Pages for
both the preacher and the listener. The insight that people need to hear the
gospel and good news regularly is an encouraging reminder for any
preacher and people. Trouble and grace are contemporary ways of stating
law and gospel, which may help present-day hearers relate to the biblical
world and the hope found in God. Wilson provides helpful guidelines for
theme sentence development. Although the Four Pages as a sermon form
may initially seem rigid, Wilson allows for flexibility.
Matters for Consideration
Chapell: In Christ-centered preaching there is the risk that all sermons
may sound the same and do not account for the biblical author’s intentions.
There does not appear to be a uniform way to preach Christ-centered
sermons since there are many different emphases (i.e., typology, Christ in
every text, the gospel in every sermon, etc.) and approaches. Chapell seeks
to guard against moralism/legalism. However, sermon application often
becomes “Jesus accomplished everything for us, we cannot obey on our
own”; therefore, the unintended consequence may be that listeners do not
need to bother trying. This raises the issue of proper emphasis on the
application of the particular text.
Kuruvilla: Guidelines for sermon construction for “christiconic”
preaching would be welcome. The biblical world needs to have more
connection to the modern world. Preachers need to know the distinction

--- Page 158 ---
between what the author is doing and what he is saying and how that makes
a difference homiletically.
Langley: Theocentric preaching seems to suggest a God-centeredness,
like Christ-centeredness, at the expense of a Trinity-centeredness. What role
does the Holy Spirit play in our hermeneutics and homiletics? Some of the
homiletical suggestions may seem a bit constrained. For example, requiring
that the main points begin with God.
Wilson: The Four Pages may seem to force a structure on the text and
therefore may not take into account the complexities of the biblical genres.
How do the four theme sentences relate to the acronym TTDNIM, including
one image and one mission? That is, how do all these components
contribute to a clear central idea?
A preacher’s hermeneutic determines his or her homiletic, and exposes
its strengths and weaknesses, flexibility and inflexibility, and advantages
and limitations. This can be seen from the various contributions made in
this book. Each contributor expresses a firm commitment to the authority of
God’s Word, a respect for the exegetical process, and a desire to help
listeners grow in Christ.
Whatever your position on hermeneutics and homiletics, we hope that
this book has stimulated your thinking about the various approaches to the
theory of preaching—and hopefully, your own preaching as well.
